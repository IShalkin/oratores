# Executive Persuasion — EXE

Persuading people who have authority, are measured on something specific, are short of time, and have heard a lot of things. The three facts that make this audience different from any other, and that every procedure here follows from:

1. **They can say yes and nothing will happen.** Their agreement is cheap and their attention is expensive. What you need is not assent but a decision they will personally carry.
2. **They are not short of information.** They are short of a defensible reason to spend their own credibility. Adding evidence to an executive who already agrees intellectually changes nothing.
3. **They are accountable to someone.** Every ask you make lands on a person who will have to explain it upward. If your ask is not explainable upward in one sentence, it will not be made.

---

## EXE-01 — Read the room's actual position

**Trigger.** Boundary 1 with a senior audience.

**Procedure.**

1. Establish what they are **measured on**. Revenue, margin, utilization, delivery, retention, client satisfaction, risk. Every ask competes with those, and an ask that does not connect to any of them is a hobby regardless of its merit.
2. Establish what they are **short of** — and it is almost never information. Usually: attention, defensible justification, someone to own it, permission to fail once, or a way to explain it to their own team without losing standing.
3. Establish what they will be **blamed for**. Executives are risk-managing agents with names attached, and the fear under most senior objections is personal exposure, not organizational risk. See the standing objection set in `REF-01`.
4. Distinguish four positions that look identical in a meeting:
   - **intellectual agreement** — they accept the argument and will not change their own behaviour
   - **delegated agreement** — they agree and will pass it to someone technical, which is a refusal with good manners
   - **conditional agreement** — they will move if a specific obstacle is removed; find out which
   - **ownership** — they will make a decision and put their name on it
   Only the fourth is the objective. The first is the usual outcome and is routinely mistaken for success.
5. Assume the room already agrees with the abstract claim. **Do not spend time proving the thing they already accept.** The gap is never "do they believe this matters"; it is "will they personally do anything, and what."
6. Name the authority precisely: what can this person decide **alone**, today, without anyone else's budget? That is the boundary of a realistic ask.

**Gates.**
- What they are measured on is named.
- What they are short of is named, and it is not information.
- The personal exposure under the resistance is identified.
- The ask fits inside their unilateral authority.

**Failure signals.**
- A case that argues the importance of the subject.
- An ask that requires the person above them to agree first.
- Success defined as "the room was engaged".
- No number anywhere that appears in their own reporting.

## EXE-02 — Message fatigue

**Trigger.** Boundary 1: the audience has heard this subject repeatedly, from several directions, for a while.

**Procedure.**

1. **Name the fatigue, accurately, first.** Not as a joke, not as an apology — as an observation they will recognize as true. An audience that hears its own state described correctly grants a hearing that enthusiasm cannot buy. See `OPN-02`.
2. Then reframe. Fatigue is not resistance to the subject; it is resistance to the **framing** of the subject. The reframe that usually holds is a shift from *whether the thing matters* to *why the current approach is not producing organizational effect.* That question is new even to a room that has heard everything about the subject itself.
3. The reliable diagnostic to hand them: the distance between **activity and capability.** Count the experiments, pilots, demos, working groups and enthusiasts, then count the processes that actually changed. The gap is real, it is checkable, and naming it is not a criticism of anyone in the room.
4. Say plainly what is not the problem. "The problem is not that we lack information." "The problem is not that people are unwilling." Ruling out the obvious explanations is what makes the real one credible.
5. Ban the vocabulary the fatigue is attached to. Every occurrence of a fashionable abstraction must be replaced with one of: a specific workflow, a specific person, a specific decision, a specific risk, a specific measurement, or a specific next action. If a sentence survives the replacement unchanged, it was not saying anything.
6. Do not fabricate the grounds for urgency. An invented deadline, a manufactured inevitability and "those who do not act will disappear" are false claims about the world, and they are also precisely what the fatigue is a defence against — using them confirms that this is another instance of the thing they are tired of. Raising *real* urgency is a different act and a legitimate one: `AUD-05` records whether the urgency in the brief is real, absent or manufactured, and syntax raises the felt version deliberately in `DLV-02`. The rule is about the claim, not about the intensity.
7. Do not compensate for fatigue with production values. A bigger stage, a slicker deck and a more dramatic opening read as a larger version of the same problem.

**Gates.**
- The fatigue is named before the case.
- The reframe moves the question, not the volume.
- Every abstraction replaced by something specific.
- No invented deadline and no claimed inevitability that is not in evidence. Where the urgency in the brief is recorded as manufactured, that is written into the piece's assumptions rather than resolved silently.

**Failure signals.**
- Any of: "this changes everything", "the future is already here", "we are at an inflection point", "adapt or be left behind", "the genie is out of the bottle".
- Opening with a definition of the thing they are tired of hearing about.
- Opening with a market-size figure.
- A logo wall as evidence.
- A talk that names the fatigue and then delivers exactly the talk they were tired of.

## EXE-03 — Authority, responsibility, and the ask

**Trigger.** The piece asks a senior audience to own something.

**Procedure.**

1. State plainly **why this is theirs** and not someone else's. The argument that works is not moral; it is structural: the thing being changed is a process they own, staffed by people they manage, measured by numbers they report. If that is not true, the ask is aimed at the wrong person and no amount of appeal fixes it.
2. Where the audience treats the subject as belonging to a specialist function, name the specific decisions that cannot be delegated: what work gets redesigned, who is accountable, what the acceptable risk is, what gets stopped to make room, and what standard the output must meet. Those are not technical decisions and cannot be made by a technical team.
3. Separate what a specialist function supplies (capability, tooling, standards, safety) from what only a line leader can supply (a real process, real people, real priority, real cover for a first failure). The ask is for the second.
4. Give them **permission to fail once**, explicitly, with a bounded scope. Most senior non-adoption is the correct response to an unbounded downside; a stated boundary and a reversible step change the calculation.
5. Make the ask explainable upward in one sentence. If they cannot justify it to their own leadership in a sentence, they will not commit to it, however convinced they are.
6. Ask for the commitment **in the room**, specifically, with a date. See `ACT-01`. A commitment deferred to "let's follow up" is a decline.
7. Never ask a senior audience to be enthusiastic. Ask them to decide something.

**Gates.**
- The structural reason the decision is theirs is stated.
- The non-delegable decisions are named.
- A risk boundary and a reversible first step exist.
- The ask is one sentence, explainable upward, with a date.

**Failure signals.**
- "We need leadership buy-in."
- An ask for sponsorship with no decision attached.
- The specialist function and the line leader asked for the same thing.
- No stated boundary — which reads as no stated limit.

## EXE-04 — Technical content at the right altitude

**Trigger.** The subject is technical and the audience is not.

**Procedure.**

1. For each technical element, fill this and present it in this order. The order is the whole procedure:

   ```
   business_problem:      (in their language, in their reporting)
   current_friction:      (what a named person does today, and how long it takes)
   human_decision:        (the judgement in that flow that matters)
   capability:            (what the technology actually does — one sentence)
   human_control:         (what stays with the person, and how)
   risk_boundary:         (what it must not do, and what enforces that)
   expected_outcome:      (the specific change)
   measurement:           (how anyone will know)
   ```

2. Calibrate the depth to the **decision**, not to the subject. Explain enough that the audience can own the decision and interrogate the claim; stop there. Two failures bracket the right level:
   - **Too deep** — architecture diagrams, model names, pipeline detail. Converts you from a leader into a technician, and the audience delegates the decision back to you.
   - **Too shallow** — the capability presented as magic. An audience with no model of how it works cannot judge when it will fail, so they will either over-trust it or refuse it, and both are your fault.
3. Say what it **cannot** do, and where it fails, before anyone asks. This is the single highest-return move available with a technical subject in front of a senior audience, and it is almost always skipped.
4. Anchor to a workflow, never to a capability. "It can summarize documents" is a feature. "The first-pass review that takes an analyst four hours takes forty minutes, and the analyst still signs it" is a decision.
5. Translate the specialized term rather than dropping it, and translate it once. See `AUD-04`.
6. Distinguish, explicitly and in these words if necessary: a **demonstration** is not a proof, a **pilot** is not a rollout, a **capability** is not a practice, and an **intention** is not a result. Executives have watched all four be conflated, and the conflation is a large part of the fatigue in `EXE-02`.
7. Show the uncertainty. A slide with only the success case, shown to the people who will carry the risk, reads as a sales pitch.

**Gates.**
- Business problem stated before mechanism, every time.
- Limits and failure modes stated unprompted.
- Every technical element ends in a measurement.
- Demo / pilot / capability / practice not conflated.

**Failure signals.**
- The architecture slide narrated.
- Vendor or model names in place of results.
- No failure mode anywhere.
- A benefit stated with no unit and no denominator.
- "It just works."

## The five-question test

The quality bar for any executive piece asking for adoption. After the talk, a leader in the audience should be able to answer all five, unaided:

1. Why is the current approach not enough?
2. What specifically is being asked of **me**?
3. Which use case will I choose first?
4. Who will be accountable for launching it?
5. How will I know whether it is actually working?

If the piece does not let them answer all five, it is not finished. This is a stronger test than any structural checklist, because a piece can satisfy every rule in this package and still fail it.

## Defaults and thresholds

| Decision | Default |
|---|---|
| Time spent proving the subject matters | as close to zero as the room allows |
| Ask scope | inside their unilateral authority |
| Ask format | one sentence, explainable upward, with a date |
| Rehearse an executive talk as if | 5 minutes, whatever the slot |
| Structure for a senior audience | conclusion first, summary front, detail behind |
| Technical depth | enough to own the decision, no more |
| Failure modes stated | unprompted, before Q&A |
| Manufactured urgency | never |
| Success criterion | a decision with a name on it, not engagement |
