# Evidence and Logos — LOG

Proof, and the discipline that keeps it honest. Two things are true at once here and the module holds both: a speech moves too fast for an audience to detect a fallacy, so investing heavily in airtight logic is often wasted — *and* every claim you make is checkable afterwards by someone with an interest in checking it. So: build the argument to look sound in the room and to survive being read in the transcript.

---

## LOG-01 — Evidence inventory and the found/built split

**Trigger.** At the start of case preparation, before any argument is written.

**Procedure.**

1. Split everything into two columns:
   - **Found** — what already exists: documents, records, measurements, contracts, published research, testimony, precedent, the client's own numbers. These only have to be *used* well.
   - **Built** — what you must construct: inference, comparison, example, analogy, projection. These have to be *invented* well.
2. For each found item, prepare the argument **against** it as well as for it. The same document can be turned either way, and your opponent has the same list.
3. Map the evidential landscape from six angles, not one: what is known · what is findable with effort · what the opposition will use · what the opposition will hide · what is genuinely unknowable · what may be fabricated. The last two are where most surprises live.
4. Rank the available evidence by force for *this* audience:

   | Rank | Kind | Why it ranks here |
   |---|---|---|
   | 1 | A specific case in this audience's own context | pragmatists will not buy without in-context references |
   | 2 | An observable workflow or process, before and after | checkable, and it names what actually changes |
   | 3 | A live demonstration | vivid; but a demo is not a proof — say so |
   | 4 | A measured outcome with its denominator | strongest numeric form |
   | 5 | Independent third-party evidence | third-party endorsement is the scarce commodity |
   | 6 | Named expert testimony, ideally against their own interest | cheap credibility if genuine |
   | 7 | Analogy from an adjacent domain | conviction, not proof — label it |
   | 8 | Vision or projection | legitimate, and must be labelled as aspiration |

5. **Weigh, do not count.** Discard trifling, defective and duplicative arguments entirely. An argument that helps a little while dragging a liability is a net loss. The case will look thinner. That is the point.

**Gates.**
- Found and built are separated.
- Every found item has its counter-argument prepared.
- The unknowable and the possibly-fabricated are named.
- Weak arguments are cut, not demoted.

**Failure signals.**
- A case built entirely on found evidence — it is exactly what the objection will attack.
- Every argument you thought of, included.
- A demonstration presented as a measurement.

## LOG-02 — Making a number land

**Trigger.** Every single time a number is stated, shown, or will be quoted.

**Procedure.**

1. Decide what the number is *for*, because the treatment differs:
   - **Attention** → pick the one startling figure. One grenade beats a dozen rounds.
   - **Comprehension** → cash the magnitude out into things the audience already knows the size of.
   - **Retention** → one identifiable person's story first, then the aggregate.
2. Never state a number without a comparator. The meaning lives entirely in the comparison, which means **whoever chose the comparison chose the conclusion**. Choose it before you know which way it points, or admit that you did not.
3. Available conversions: *X is the same as Y*; scale up to something assumed larger; scale down into the listener's own week; rescale to human intuition (per day, per person, per shift); the power of one.
4. For credibility *and* recall, do both in sequence: state the exact figure precisely, then restate it rounded and pictured. Precision buys belief; rounding buys memory; neither buys both.
5. **One statistic per claim.** Two statistics on one claim tells the audience you do not know which is stronger. A pile of numbers survives nothing.
6. Never present an aggregate statistic immediately after an appeal about one person. Combining them measurably *reduces* the response, because it switches the audience from feeling into calculating. If you want feeling, do not prime counting.
7. Never end on a number. Statistics settle exchanges; they do not settle the argument. Bolt the figure to the value claim it serves.

**Gates.**
- Every number has a stated comparator.
- The comparator is named, with the point at which it was chosen — before the direction was known, or after and admitted.
- One statistic per claim.
- No aggregate immediately following a personal appeal.

**Failure signals.**
- "A lot of money", "significant savings", "substantial uplift".
- A percentage with no denominator: a target of "90% quality" says nothing about what is measured, on which slice, by whom, or how many cases would be needed to distinguish 90% from 85%.
- A number you cannot source when asked in Q&A.
- A rounded figure presented as precise, or a precise figure that implies a precision the method cannot support.
- Any ROI or benchmark you cannot point at. If the figure does not exist, say the figure does not exist.

## LOG-03 — Argument construction and order

**Trigger.** Building the proof section.

**Procedure.**

1. Build inferences the audience completes themselves. Find a premise they **already hold** — about what usually happens, or a fact that points your way — join it to your claim, and **delete** the premise they can supply. The omission is the source of the force, not a defect. Do not reason from too far back: the chain must be short enough to hold in mind.
2. Argue the **future** by precedent and example; argue the **past** by inference. This is a hard constraint, not a preference: when arguing what to do, you have no facts about the outcome, only parallels.
3. **Placement decides how many examples you need, so decide the placement first.** An example placed *before* the claim is doing induction — the audience is being asked to generalize from it, and one instance is a weak generalization, so you need several. An example placed *after* the claim is doing the work of a witness, and witnesses are weighed rather than counted, so one good one is enough. Where you have no inference to offer, the examples have to carry the proof themselves: they go first, in numbers. Where you have one, they follow it as corroboration, and a queue of them there only spends time. Taking those two decisions in the wrong order produces the two commonest defects — a single example leading, and a crowd of them trailing.
4. Advance the case on several routes so it accumulates: knowledge, authority, probability, direct experience, the opposition's own words, and analogy. Stack at least three; a case advanced on one route is one refutation from collapse.
5. Order the proofs. The literature holds two positions here and the condition that selects between them is known, so choose rather than default.

   **Strongest first**, one strong argument held in reserve for the close — where attention is not guaranteed. The room may leave or stop listening, you are one of several speakers, or the piece has to survive being cut short. A case that does not look like the better one at the opening is in bad condition.

   **Ascend to the strongest**, opening on a lesser point — where the audience is captive and obliged to decide at the end. The decision is taken after you finish and the last position is best remembered; leading with your peak inverts the climb of attention and leaves the remaining points reading as padding.

   Both agree on the middle: bury the moderate ones. On the weak ones they part. One line cuts them entirely; the other places a necessary-but-weak argument **between two strong ones**, so it is carried rather than exposed. Cut it if it is optional, sandwich it if you must make it.
6. Where the evidence cannot decide, do not contest facts head-on. Build the general framework that unifies and magnifies your probabilities, and make the credibility of the opposing account the major proof.
7. Where the audience arrives with prejudgements, **address the opinions before the facts** — name them, label them as misconceptions, and only then reappraise the evidence. Arguing facts into a prejudged room is wasted.
8. **Vary the handling of every proof.** Finding the argument is not enough; how each one is worked is a separate decision, and working them all the same way both tires the audience and exposes the machinery. The available handlings: state the proposition and then show why it holds; state it and let the reasoning close it; make no proposition at all and let the reasoning show what proposition could have been made; and, where the proof is a comparison, prepare the comparison before you produce it.

   **On one of those handlings the literature disagrees, and it does not supply the condition that settles it.**

   **Draw the conclusion yourself** — the stated default, on the ground that you cannot risk the audience reaching the wrong one. This position concedes the other's mechanism: a conclusion the audience reaches itself is held more durably, and it declines to rely on it anyway.

   **Leave it to the hearer** — on the ground that the audience that draws the conclusion owns it, and as one of the handlings you rotate so that no two proofs are worked identically.

   Both reasons are stated. Neither line states which proofs get which treatment, or how high the wrong-conclusion risk has to be to override. So decide it proof by proof and write down which you chose; a piece that draws every conclusion, or withholds every one, is running a habit rather than a decision. This is a different question from step 1 — deleting a premise the audience can supply is not the same act as withholding the conclusion, and a piece can do the first everywhere while doing the second nowhere.

   Two cases are already settled elsewhere and are not open here. **On a slide or a chart the conclusion is always stated**, in the title and in words on the visual — `VIS-02`, `DAT-03`. And where a story carries a counter-intuitive verdict, the audience reaches it a beat before you say it, and you still say it — `NAR-03`. What is open is the spoken and written proof. Those two exceptions are not the literature settling this dispute by medium — neither line engaged with the other. They are separately sourced rules that happen to decide their own cases, and this package routes each to the module that owns it. Where a rule of yours covers a case, it is not evidence that the disagreement above has an answer.
9. Do not run proof continuously. Alternate with character and feeling, or the audience stops following regardless of quality.

**Gates.**
- At least three independent routes to the conclusion.
- Example placement decided before the count, and the count matched to it.
- A proof order chosen against the attention condition and written down, not defaulted; one strong argument held for the close; any weak argument either cut or deliberately sandwiched.
- Prejudgements handled before facts, where they exist.
- The handling varied across proofs, and the conclusion-drawing decided per proof and written down.

**Failure signals.**
- One long chain where every link must hold.
- The best argument at the end of the middle section.
- A single example leading a section — a generalization from one instance.
- A queue of examples after a proof that one of them would have carried.
- Every proof worked identically: proposition, evidence, conclusion, next.
- Uninterrupted argument for more than a couple of minutes.

## LOG-04 — Claim discipline and the fallacy screen

**Trigger.** Every draft, before it leaves your hands. Mandatory when the piece is attributable, regulated, or will be quoted.

**Procedure.**

1. Label every material claim on two axes. The first is the **type** — what kind of assertion it is:

   | Type | Means |
   |---|---|
   | `FACT` | the case now, and checkable in principle |
   | `OBSERVATION` | we saw this, in this context, n times |
   | `INTERPRETATION` | our reading of the facts; a reasonable person could read differently |
   | `FORECAST` | a projection, with its assumptions stated |
   | `HYPOTHESIS` | what we think is true and have not tested |
   | `ASPIRATION` | what we intend or hope; not evidence of anything |

   Never let a later category wear the clothes of an earlier one. Most dishonesty in professional communication is not a false fact; it is a forecast delivered in the grammar of a fact.

   The second is the **status of the source** — whether anybody checked it. Each status carries its one-line reason:

   | Status | Means |
   |---|---|
   | `CHECKED` | checked against the source, with a locator you could produce |
   | `UNVERIFIED` | not checked; it is in the draft on that basis |
   | `CONTRADICTED` | the source says something else, or two sources disagree |
   | `RECHECK` | it was checked, and something it depends on has since changed |
   | `NOT_APPLICABLE` | the warrant is your own reasoning or intent; there is no external source to check |

   The axes are independent. The type does not mean the claim has been verified, so `FACT` with `UNVERIFIED` is a real state, it is the common one, and it is the one worth catching before delivery. Softening the type to cover a source nobody could check — a fact restated as an interpretation, an observation restated as a hypothesis — relabels the claim as something it is not and hides the defect that was actually there. The type is not where doubt is recorded; the status is. `NOT_APPLICABLE` is a statement about the claim and not an excuse for it: an interpretation, a forecast, a hypothesis and an aspiration have no external source anybody could check, so each carries its reasoning and its assumptions instead. Reach for it only where no source could settle the claim — a fact nobody got round to checking is `UNVERIFIED`, and recording it as not applicable is the same evasion as softening the type.
2. Every claim must be accurate, relevant, current, and **attributable**. Attribution is cheap; make it: name the source in the sentence.
3. Screen for the fallacies that actually appear in drafts: hasty generalization · straw man · person instead of position · false cause · post hoc · comparing unlike things · non sequitur · "they do it too" · false binary · slippery slope · appeal to popularity · defining the term so you win.
4. The usual fixes, none of which cost you the applause line: replace "some say" with a real quotation; add a qualifier where the claim is a probability; put the evidence before the motive claim; make the compared contexts comparable.
5. Never present a count as coverage. A green suite means previously-found problems stay fixed; it says nothing about the class nobody has thought of. Same for reach: an exposure number is not an effect.
6. When something in a delivered piece turns out to be false: **correct the record explicitly, once, and do not repeat the claim.** Not correcting is the failure that compounds.

**Gates.**
- Every material claim carries a type and a source status, and the status carries its reason.
- Nothing is delivered as `CHECKED` without a locator you could produce.
- No forecast phrased as a fact.
- Fallacy screen run.
- Every number and quotation has a locator you could produce.

**Failure signals.**
- "Studies show", "research indicates", "industry benchmarks suggest" with no named study.
- A projected benefit stated in the past tense.
- A pilot described as if it were a rollout, or an intention described as a capability.
- A claim's type softened because nobody could verify the source — a fact restated as an interpretation to make the missing locator look deliberate. The type was the honest part; the status was the part that was missing.
- "We won't dignify that" or "no comment" in a prepared answer — reads as guilt plus ineptitude.

## LOG-05 — Argument you can lose well

**Trigger.** High-stakes, contested, or adversarial settings.

**Procedure.**

1. Decide what is actually achievable and aim there. Sometimes protecting credibility beats winning the exchange, and a piece that overreaches costs you the next three.
2. Prepare a **surplus** of argument, so sections can be dropped or reordered when the room turns, and rehearse it that way. Master the case well enough that anything can be moved.
3. Prepare the answers to the questions you cannot control before you prepare the ones you can.
4. Concede genuine ignorance once, plainly, and offer to follow up. It costs almost nothing and buys the rest of the answers.
5. Never debate a questioner. Answer, and move on. See `REF-04`.

**Gates.**
- The achievable objective is named, and it may not be "win".
- Surplus material exists.
- The ignorance concession is pre-authorized.

**Failure signals.**
- A single script with no drop-outs.
- No prepared answer for the strongest question against you.
- An answer that argues with the questioner rather than the room.

## Defaults and thresholds

| Decision | Default |
|---|---|
| Statistics per claim | 1 |
| Independent argument routes | ≥ 3 |
| Proof order | chosen against the attention condition and written down; one strong argument held for the close |
| A necessary but weak argument | cut, or placed between two strong ones — the sources part here, so decide and record which |
| Every number | exact figure + rounded picture, in that order |
| Claims requiring a label | all material ones |
| Aggregate after a personal appeal | never |
| Ending on a number | never |
| Correction of a false claim | once, explicitly, no repetition |
