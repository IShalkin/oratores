# Openings and Closings — OPN

The two positions that are worth disproportionate effort, for two different reasons. The opening is the only stretch where you are guaranteed undivided attention. The closing is the only stretch that is guaranteed to be remembered. A dull piece with a strong close beats a strong piece that ends flat.

Both should be written **last** and rehearsed **hardest**. Openings drafted first are reliably thin, because you do not yet know what you are opening.

---

## OPN-01 — The opening

**Trigger.** Every piece. Draft it after the body exists.

**Procedure.**

1. Decide which of the six jobs this opening must do — it will rarely be all of them:
   - establish common ground
   - set the tone
   - establish credibility
   - arouse interest
   - use the guaranteed attention while it lasts
   - get into the subject
   A device that does none of these is an interruption, however charming.
2. **Move the amenities out.** Opening pleasantries are opening inanities: they spend the only guaranteed attention you have on the only content nobody needs. Thanks and praise belong in the **middle**, where the same words read as sincerity rather than as flattery — wedge them in by apparent free association.
3. Choose the category deliberately:

   | Situation | Opening |
   |---|---|
   | Grave occasion, stakes self-evident | acknowledge, then straight to the point |
   | You want them leaning forward | a stark fact, a startling statement, or suspense |
   | The audience is tired of the subject | name the fatigue accurately, then say what is actually different |
   | You have a personal story about the topic | use it; there is no better beginning |
   | The audience does not know why they should care | a surprising question, or a concrete image |
   | You have a working object or capability | tease it, then earn it |
   | Nothing else applies | a **reference** — the default, and the most common |

4. Build a reference opening by walking the inventory: the date, the place, the subject, the organization, a historical or current event, the introduction you were just given, the title, the speaker's own history with this room. Then apply the **familiarity test** for *this* audience and state the tie-in explicitly. A reference outside their world lands as silence.
5. Where you have nothing local, one hour of research buys attention and likability at once: an anniversary of the date, something specific to the place, the thing this audience has that nobody else does.
6. Win two wars in sequence. The first ten seconds buys attention; the first minute buys the journey. You can withhold the reveal, but keep the audience **mapped** — they must know roughly where they are going even while the surprise is held.
7. **Never open with a joke.** Humour depends on surprise, and an audience told to expect funny is the one condition under which funny cannot happen. Convert the joke into an anecdote and move it to the middle, where a laugh is a bonus rather than a requirement.
8. Do not open on "what could be". Establish the audience's current reality in their own terms first, so they nod before you ask them to move.
9. **Write the title last, and pick its job.** A **descriptive** title tells the audience what they are about to get, and is the right choice where the subject compels attention on its own. An **intriguing** title exists to make a dry subject worth walking into, and is the right choice where it does not. Either is defensible; drifting into one is not. Keep it fresh, short, suited to the subject, and interesting enough to be read twice. Then the constraint, which falls on the opening rather than on the title: however oblique the title, the piece ties it to the subject **inside the first few minutes**. Held past that, intrigue stops working and the audience guesses instead of listening. This is the title of the piece; a slide title is a different object and always asserts its conclusion — `VIS-02`. `INV-03` generates the title candidates; this step chooses between them.
10. If the draft still opens with the placeholder you typed to get started, find the most interesting thing in the body, cut it, and paste it at the top.

**Gates.**
- Which of the six jobs the opening does is written down, and the jobs it deliberately leaves undone are named.
- No amenities in the first thirty seconds.
- Every reference passes the familiarity test and has an explicit tie-in.
- The title is written after the piece, its job named, and any oblique title tied to the subject inside the opening.
- Scripted and memorized verbatim.

**Failure signals.**
- "It's a pleasure to be here." "Thank you to the organizers." "Let me start with a definition."
- A long statistic as the first content.
- An opening joke.
- An opening whose relevance you have to explain later.
- A title written before the piece it names, or explained on the last page.
- A hostile or fatigued room addressed as though it were neutral.

## OPN-02 — Acknowledging fatigue and skepticism

**Trigger.** The audience has heard this subject many times, or arrives disbelieving. This is common enough to deserve its own procedure. See also `EXE-02`.

**Procedure.**

1. Say it. Name the fatigue plainly and accurately — not as a self-deprecating joke, but as an observation the audience will recognize as true. An audience that hears its own actual state described will grant you a hearing that no amount of enthusiasm buys.
2. Then earn the next sentence by naming what is **actually different**, in a form they can check. The difference is almost never "the technology has improved". It is usually a specific change in what is now possible, cheap, required, or already happening in a place they recognize.
3. Do not manufacture urgency. False deadlines and invented inevitability are exactly what the fatigue is a defence against, and they confirm it.
4. Avoid the openings that signal you have not understood the room: a definition of the thing they are tired of hearing about; a market-size statistic; an assertion that the future has arrived; a slide of logos.
5. Available honest openings for a fatigued room, in rough order of safety:
   - the gap between the number of experiments and the number of things that actually changed
   - a short account of something that was tried and did not work, and why
   - a concrete scene from the audience's own work, described accurately
   - a question about the audience's own responsibility, asked without accusation
   - an observation that contradicts the standard framing and is checkable
   - a paradox the audience will recognize as their own
6. For each candidate opening, state to yourself: why it should hold attention, what its risk is, which segment it suits, and what would make it stronger. Then pick one. Do not stack two.

**Gates.**
- The fatigue is named before the case is made.
- The stated difference is checkable.
- No manufactured urgency anywhere.

**Failure signals.**
- Enthusiasm as the answer to skepticism.
- "This time is different" with nothing behind it.
- Naming the fatigue and then delivering exactly the talk they were tired of.

## OPN-03 — The close

**Trigger.** Every piece. The last thirty seconds are always planned and always memorized.

**Procedure.**

1. Choose the **emotion** first, then the vehicle. The four that actually work in professional settings:

   | Emotion | Vehicle | Use when |
   |---|---|---|
   | Pride | what this group has already done that was hard | a company, industry, craft or team piece |
   | Hope | the specific future the decision opens | a new product, plan, reorganization, or capability |
   | Love / loyalty | a person, a family, an obligation | ceremonial, and in ordinary business talks where patriotism would be too much |
   | Fear | the consequence of not moving | sparingly, and only where the consequence is real and the action is named |

2. Pick a close type, and usually combine two:
   - **summary** — restate the argument
   - **circle** — return to the opening image
   - **direct appeal** — ask for the order
   - **thesis** — restate the governing idea in its sharpest form
   - **reference** — the occasion, the place, the date
   - **inspirational** — the objective correlative: sacrifice, long odds, someone who did it first
   - **anecdotal** — one story that carries the whole point
3. For an advocacy piece, run the four-part close in order: **inspiration → the lesson it teaches → the call to action → the clincher.** Omit the clincher and the piece ends abruptly; omit the call and nothing happens.
4. Turn the call to action back on the speaker. A commitment the speaker makes first is worth more than an instruction, and it is what makes the ask credible.
5. Load the close with the figures you rationed elsewhere. This is where they belong.
6. **Never signal that you are about to stop.** "In closing", "before I finish", "I have just a minute left" — each one discharges the attention you were about to use.
7. Then stop. One short line after the emotional peak, and stop. Do not end on a video, a sponsor slide, an apology, a pitch, or "any questions?"
8. Reduce the close to a **symbol prompt** — one word or drawn mark at the foot of the page — so it can be delivered looking at the audience rather than at the paper.
9. Where the piece must be timed, build two endings: a summary false ending and the real close. If you are running long, take the first.

**Gates.**
- Emotion chosen before vehicle.
- Call to action present and specific if the piece asks for anything.
- Memorized; deliverable looking up.
- No ending signal anywhere.

**Failure signals.**
- A correct but limp final sentence — end one line earlier.
- A cliché close.
- Mixed metaphors in the last paragraph, which is where they cluster.
- "Thank you. Any questions?" as the actual ending.
- A close that reinforces a different point from the thesis.

## Defaults and thresholds

| Decision | Default |
|---|---|
| Opening drafted | last |
| Title | written last; job named; tied to the subject inside the opening |
| Opening and close | scripted verbatim and memorized |
| Amenities in the opening | none; move them to the middle |
| Attention windows | 10 seconds for attention, 1 minute for the journey |
| Opening joke | never |
| Ending signal | never |
| Endings prepared | 2 — a summary false ending and the real one |
| Close reduced to | a one-word or symbol prompt |
