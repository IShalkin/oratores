# Executive Technology-Adoption Activation

An address to a large, senior, message-fatigued audience whose intellectual agreement is already available and whose behaviour has not changed. The deliverable is not a speech. It is an **activation system**: a talk, plus the commitments it extracts, plus the mechanism that carries those commitments into business units and back with results.

Written against AI adoption because that is where the pattern is currently sharpest. It applies unchanged to any technology or practice where the audience has heard a great deal, seen many pilots, and changed little.

---

## When this applies

All of the following:

- The audience holds real authority — P&L owners, managing directors, country or practice heads, partners, business-unit leads.
- They have heard this subject many times, from several directions, over a sustained period.
- They largely **agree** with the abstract claim and have not changed their own behaviour.
- The objective is organizational adoption — a real process changed, in their unit, by their people — rather than approval, awareness, or enthusiasm.
- The talk is one event in something larger, or should be.

## When this does not apply

- The audience genuinely lacks information. That is an explanation task; use `MSG-02` and `EXE-04` and skip most of this.
- The audience is hostile to the technology on grounds of principle rather than fatigued by the messaging. Use `REF-03`; the fatigue reframe will read as evasion.
- You are being asked for an inspirational keynote and adoption is not the objective. Say so, and do not build this instead of what was asked.
- The organization has not decided to do the thing. This playbook activates a decision; it does not substitute for one, and using it to manufacture a mandate that does not exist is `MEC-02`.

## Required inputs

**Where an answer can reach you**, ask at most seven, ranked by how much the answer changes the work. Where refused, use the default and state the assumption at the top of the deliverable — then produce the complete work anyway. **Where it cannot** — a forked run, a background task, any non-interactive context — do not ask at all: every row below counts as refused, take every default, and put the assumptions in a block at the top. This playbook is reached through the `exec-activation` fork more often than not, so treat that as the ordinary case rather than the exception.

| Input | Default if refused |
|---|---|
| Duration and slot position | 20 minutes plus 10 of questions; assume mid-programme |
| Audience size and composition | large (hundreds), mixed seniority above business-unit lead |
| Language and register | the organization's working language; professional, unadorned, low-theatre |
| Who the speaker is, and their standing with this room | a senior insider with authority but not line authority over the audience |
| The single behaviour wanted afterwards | one named workflow selected, one owner appointed, one bounded pilot launched, one baseline measured, reported in 30 days |
| The technical case with a real workflow | flag as the **blocking gap**; the piece cannot be finished without one, and a generic capability description will produce exactly the talk they are tired of |
| Evidence actually available, and what may be cited | assume only internal observation, unpublished; label everything accordingly and use no external figures |
| How public and attributable | internal, recorded, quotable inside the organization |
| Emotional latitude | restrained; pride and hope available, fear not |

**One input is genuinely blocking — in an interactive run, ask for it first; in a fork, specify it as a gap and build everything else:** a real workflow, in this audience's own work, with a real friction and a real measurement. Everything else has a defensible default. Without that one, say so plainly and deliver everything that does not depend on it, with the technical layer left as a specified gap rather than filled with generic material.

## The objective block

Fill this before writing anything. It is the artifact stage 3 produces.

```
audience:
situation:
core_problem:
strategic_tension:
desired_understanding:
desired_belief:
desired_emotion:
desired_behavior:
organizational_outcome:
success_metric:
first_report_date:
```

The distinction that this block exists to enforce, and that most executive talks collapse: what they must **understand** is different from what they must **believe**, which is different from what they must **feel**, which is different from what they must **do**. This audience usually understands and believes already. The failure is at feel and do — which means most of the material that would ordinarily be written is unnecessary here, and the material that is missing is the mechanism.

## The communication position

The governing move for this genre. Not the words — the position.

Do **not** argue that the technology matters. Assume that argument was won, by someone else, some time ago, and that repeating it is the thing the audience is tired of.

Argue instead about the **gap between activity and capability**: that the organization has no shortage of experiments, pilots, demonstrations, enthusiasts or working groups, and a shortage of processes that actually changed — and that closing that gap is a leadership problem rather than a technology problem, because the things it requires are things only a line leader can supply.

Something in the shape of: *the question is no longer whether we should be doing this; it is whether we can turn a set of scattered experiments into a managed capability embedded in real processes, real client work and real decisions.*

Do not copy that sentence. It is a direction. Write the version that is true of this organization, in this audience's language, with this organization's numbers, and make it specific enough that it could be wrong.

### Vocabulary that is banned in this genre

Every occurrence must be replaced by one of: a specific workflow · a specific person · a specific decision · a specific risk · a specific measurement · a specific next action. A sentence that survives the replacement unchanged was not saying anything.

Banned outright: *AI will change everything* · *the future is already here* · *adapt or be left behind* · *we're at an inflection point* · *the genie is out of the bottle* · *this is not the future, it's the present* · *unlock* · *unleash* · *reimagine* · *transformation journey* · *at pace* · *at scale* (unqualified) · *game-changing* · *revolution* · *disruption* (unless naming a specific displaced process).

Also banned as **openings**: a definition of the technology · a market-size figure · a vendor or model comparison · a logo wall · a video · anything that requires the audience to already believe in the technology.

## The deliverable

Produce these sections, in this order. Length scales with the occasion; the sequence does not change.

1. **Strategic brief** — the objective block, plus what the audience is measured on, short of, and exposed to. Assumptions stated.
2. **Audience map** — behavioural segments, each with what they think, want, fear, distrust, what evidence they accept, what action is realistic, and the language to use and to avoid. `AUD-02`.
3. **Communication objective** — understand / believe / feel / do, separated, with the metric and the report date.
4. **Core thesis** — one sentence, run against the `MSG-01` tests and the five-question test.
5. **Key message architecture** — three strategic principles at most, each with the claim, the proof, and the objection it pre-empts.
6. **Speech structure** — sections in order. For each: purpose, key claim, emotional function, logical function, evidence, transition out, expected audience reaction. `MSG-05`.
7. **Opening options** — three to five candidates. For each: why it should hold attention, its risk, which segment it suits, how to strengthen it. Recommend one. `OPN-02`.
8. **Main narrative** — the drafted body, or the beat-level structure if a draft was not requested. One human or client case. `NAR-01`.
9. **Technical explanation** — the eight-field template per element, in order, at the altitude that lets them own the decision. `EXE-04`.
10. **Objection map** — seven fields per objection, drawn from the standing set in `REF-01` and narrowed to this organization.
11. **Evidence plan** — the hierarchy, each item labelled, with the gaps named as gaps. `LOG-01`, `LOG-04`.
12. **Leadership commitment** — the six questions and the 30-day block. `ACT-01`.
13. **Top-down cascade** — the chain with an owner per link, and what each carrying leader receives, says, is supported by, needs removed, and publishes. `ACT-03`.
14. **Slide-by-slide** — per slide: number, conclusion-as-title, purpose, single message, visual, narration, the question it answers, transition. One action slide. `VIS-01`–`VIS-03`.
15. **Delivery plan** — pace, pauses, emphasis, movement, staging for a large room, the demonstration and its failure plan. `DLV-01`, `DLV-03`.
16. **Rehearsal plan** — passes, timing, the ranked cut list, who reviews it, what gets recorded. `DLV-02`, `DLV-04`.
17. **Q&A preparation** — the mundane, the technical, the hostile, and the one nobody will ask aloud. `REF-04`.
18. **Risk and exposure review** — `MEC-03` in full: what is being claimed versus what is known, which claim gets checked first, and what it costs if it does not hold.
19. **Quality assessment** — the audit below, with findings ranked worst-first and anchored.
20. **Missing inputs** — what you did not have, what you assumed, and what would change if the assumption is wrong.

## Genre-specific failure modes

The general modules warn about the general failures. These are the ones specific to this genre, in rough order of how often they occur.

1. **Winning an argument nobody is having.** Half the talk spent establishing that the technology matters, to a room that already agreed and is bored of hearing it. The single most common failure here.
2. **Intellectual agreement mistaken for success.** The room is engaged, the feedback is warm, nothing changes. Engagement is not the metric; a named owner and a date is.
3. **The delegated yes.** "Great — let's get the technology team on it." This is a refusal with good manners, and it is the default outcome unless `EXE-03` names the non-delegable decisions explicitly.
4. **An ask outside their authority.** Anything needing a budget decision or a peer's agreement is a referral, not a commitment.
5. **Manufactured urgency.** Invented deadlines and existential framing confirm that this is another instance of the thing they are tired of. See `EXE-02`.
6. **Production values substituting for a case.** A bigger stage and a slicker deck read as a larger version of the same problem.
7. **The architecture slide.** Boxes and arrows narrated to a business audience, which converts the speaker into a technician and hands the decision back. See `VIS-04`.
8. **A demo presented as a proof.** This audience has watched all four of demo / pilot / capability / practice be conflated, and the conflation is a large part of the fatigue. Label them. See `LOG-04`.
9. **An unbounded downside.** Senior non-adoption is usually the correct response to a risk nobody has bounded. State the boundary and the reversible step, and the calculation changes.
10. **Contempt for the sceptics.** Characterizing the resistant as behind, frightened or not yet understanding costs the room, and it discards the best available information about the real obstacle. Several of the standing objections are correct.
11. **A talk with no mechanism.** Inspiration with nothing attached. The most avoidable failure in the list, and it is designed in at the outline stage.
12. **No cascade artifact.** Nobody converts a speech into a plan on the way home. Without the one-page sheet, the selection criteria, the boundary template and the reporting format, the cascade is a hope.
13. **Only positive results published.** Teaches the organization that the reporting is decorative, which costs more than the failures did. See `ACT-03`.
14. **Fabricated numbers.** An unsourced return figure or an anonymous "research shows" in a room that contains one informed person, which it will. See `MEC-03`.
15. **A mandate dressed as a consensus.** Presenting a decision already taken as though the room were choosing, or claiming to speak for a workforce that was not asked. See `MEC-02`.

## Quality bar

Two tests. Both must pass. The second is stronger than any checklist in this package.

**Test 1 — the audit.** Run `REV-03` in full: strategic clarity, audience fit, persuasive completeness, narrative, actionability, exposure. Findings worst-first, each anchored, each with the smallest fix.

**Test 2 — the five questions.** A leader in this audience must be able to answer all five, unaided, on the way out:

1. Why is the current approach not enough?
2. What specifically is being asked of **me**?
3. Which use case will I choose first?
4. Who will be accountable for launching it?
5. How will I know whether it is actually working?

If the piece does not let them answer all five, it is not finished — regardless of how well it satisfies everything else. Rework it rather than shipping it.

**One practical note on the sceptics**, offered as mechanics rather than as a rule. This genre's characteristic trap is a talk that leaves dissenters no way to voice an objection without looking like they are behind. It reliably produces a warm room and, four months later, quiet non-implementation — because the objection did not go away, it went underground, where you cannot answer it and cannot even find it. Several of the standing objections in `REF-01` are correct, and they are the cheapest information about the real obstacle you will get. Whether you want that information is a judgement about this audience, and it is yours.
