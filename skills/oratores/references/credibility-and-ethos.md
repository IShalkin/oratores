# Credibility and Ethos — ETH

The most authoritative of the three proofs, and the one most people leave to chance. The operative constraint that makes it a craft rather than a circumstance: **credibility has to be produced by the piece itself.** Reputation you arrive with is useful and is not the art. What the audience concludes about your character *from how you speak* is.

---

## ETH-01 — The credibility diagnostic

**Trigger.** The audience is not accepting an argument you know is sound.

**Procedure.** Credibility has three components. A failure of belief traces to a defect in exactly one of them. Diagnose which before repairing anything.

| What the audience is thinking | Missing component | The repair |
|---|---|---|
| "You've got this wrong" | **Judgement** — do you know what you are talking about | Show command of the specifics; concede the genuinely uncertain; name the limits of your own case |
| "You're spinning me" | **Honesty** — are you telling me the truth | Concede a point against your own interest. Never assert your own honesty; the assertion is evidence against it |
| "You don't care about us" | **Goodwill** — are you on my side | State their interest, in their terms, before yours |

Notes that decide the repair:

1. Repair only the missing one. Adding evidence to a goodwill problem makes it worse.
2. Goodwill repairs are cheap and fast. Honesty repairs are slow and cannot be asserted — only demonstrated, by what you concede.
3. **Volunteering a real, secondary weakness before your main claim is the cheapest credibility available.** It must be genuine and it must be secondary; a manufactured one is detected. Arguing briefly against your own interest is the same move.
4. Where your credibility is weak: milder tone, non-confrontational language, gradual introduction of the difficult material, and nothing that further alienates. Where it is strong: still prepare fully — standing invites the impression of arrogance, which is a credibility defect of its own.

**Gates.**
- The missing component is identified before anything is added.
- At least one genuine concession against interest exists in the draft.
- No sentence asserts the speaker's own honesty, objectivity or good faith.

**Failure signals.**
- "Let me be honest with you", "to be completely transparent", "I'll be candid".
- A draft with no concession anywhere.
- Credentials recited at length in the opening — this reads as insecurity, not authority.

## ETH-02 — Constructing standing you do not have

**Trigger.** The speaker is unknown to this audience, junior to it, or outside its guild.

**Procedure.**

1. Convert the credibility deficit into a differentiator rather than hiding it. An outsider position, a non-standard background, or a practitioner's view against an institutional one are all assets if named deliberately and once.
2. Use the **hardest relevant thing you have actually done**, stated concretely, in place of a track record the audience would recognize. One mission-critical case that maps onto their problem does more than a list. The requirement is that you actually have one; a near-miss invites the comparison you will lose.
3. Where the audience trusts itself more than it trusts you, hand them a **checkable claim**: "look at your own numbers for last quarter", "ask your team how long that step takes". A falsifiable challenge outperforms any statistic you could cite — and it cedes control of the verdict, which is why it works.
4. Prefer a source with lived experience and nothing to gain over a source with status. Where the audience is asking what you have to gain, institutional polish is the wrong currency.
5. Borrow standing legitimately: named third parties, in-context references from people like the audience, and — strongest of all — the opponent's or the critic's own words in your favour. Borrowed authority collapses entirely if payment or access is later exposed, so use only what you can disclose.
6. Where you speak *for* a group, the claim is only as good as the group's willingness to be spoken for. See `MEC-02`.

**Gates.**
- The deficit is named once, deliberately, and turned.
- At least one checkable claim the audience can verify themselves.
- No borrowed authority you could not disclose the basis of.

**Failure signals.**
- Padding the introduction with credentials.
- "Industry leaders agree" with no leaders named.
- An endorsement whose commercial basis is not disclosable.

## ETH-03 — Congruence

**Trigger.** Always. Check before wording.

**Procedure.**

1. **Words and actions must agree.** When they do not, the audience concludes oblique at best and dishonest at worst — and no amount of communication programme outweighs one senior person behaving inconsistently with the message. Fix the congruence before you fix the wording.
2. Say only what the speaker can actually believe. Only the convinced convince, and conviction cannot be supplied by a writer. If the speaker does not hold the position, that is a `ETH-04` problem, not a drafting problem.
3. Do not attempt total candour. There is almost always material that cannot be said, and pretending otherwise produces the specific disaster of a leader saying the true thing that ends them. What is available instead is honest *restraint*: say less, say it plainly, and do not claim to be saying everything.
4. **Deeds are the strongest channel.** Three visible, retellable actions consistent with the message do more than the message. Where the piece asks for a behaviour, the speaker should already be doing it, and should say so factually rather than virtuously.
5. Feel the passage yourself before delivering it. Simulated feeling is worse than none — see `PTH-03`.
6. Where the speaker is indifferent to the topic, send them back to the material rather than rewriting the draft — no wording repairs a speaker with nothing at stake. The usable test: ask what they would say if the decision went the wrong way. A speaker who cannot answer has not found their own stake in it yet, and the fix is another pass at the evidence, not another pass at the words.

**Gates.**
- No claim in the piece is contradicted by the speaker's own known behaviour.
- Nothing in the draft the speaker cannot say sincerely.
- Any behaviour the piece asks for, the speaker is already doing or explicitly commits to.

**Failure signals.**
- A leader thumping the lectern about humility.
- A message about focus delivered in a talk with eleven points.
- An "engagement session" that takes no questions.
- Rhetoric far ahead of the record — this sets up a reversal that damages you retrospectively.

## ETH-04 — Writing for someone else

**Trigger.** The speaker is not the author: a principal, a client, an executive, a candidate.

**Procedure.**

1. **The speaker is always right about their own voice.** Your job is not to win the argument about the wording; it is to produce something they will deliver well. A brilliant line they cannot say is worth nothing.
2. Interview them **adversarially**: argue the opposite case and see what they defend. This surfaces two things a straight interview does not — what they actually believe, and how they actually talk under pressure.
3. Collect their register: sentence length, favoured words, the words they never use, how they open, whether they can land a joke. Then write inside it. Aim for the final text to be mostly *their* register, not yours.
4. Never let the piece sound like the writer. A borrowed voice is audible, and it is audible first to the audience the speaker knows best.
5. Never send an unrehearsed line. The speaker should cut, rearrange and say it aloud until they own it; a line delivered without having been made their own reads as read.
6. Manage the review process explicitly. Committee review reliably dilutes a piece — everyone has a fork. Keep a single owner for the text, take substantive notes, and refuse edits that break the thesis or the structure without saying so.
7. **Disclose, do not dismiss, on ghostwriting.** The convention is well understood; the professional posture is to acknowledge it plainly when asked rather than to deny it. What is not acceptable is presenting borrowed *ideas* or borrowed *wording* as original: attribute cheaply and often, and treat a distinctive phrase as needing credit even when a paraphrase would not.
8. Where the speaker's position and the honest case diverge, say so once, plainly, in the brief — and then write the piece they have decided to give. It is their platform and their accountability. The exception is a claim you would have to fabricate; that is `MEC-03`, and it is not a matter of deference.

**Gates.**
- An adversarial interview happened, or its absence is flagged as a risk.
- The final register is recognizably the speaker's.
- Every borrowed phrase or idea is attributed.
- Divergences between the speaker's position and the evidence are stated in the brief.

**Failure signals.**
- The principal explains the case better in the room than the draft does.
- Vocabulary the speaker has never used.
- A draft returned with a tick and no marks — they did not read it.
- A joke supplied to a speaker who cannot land one. Send none, or send four short ones as insurance.

## Defaults and thresholds

| Decision | Default |
|---|---|
| Concessions against interest | ≥ 1 per piece, genuine and secondary |
| Assertions of your own honesty | 0 |
| Credentials in an opening | 3 at most, implying more; better still, move them out |
| Writer's share of the final register | as low as possible |
| Lines delivered unrehearsed | 0 |
| Borrowed phrases unattributed | 0 |
