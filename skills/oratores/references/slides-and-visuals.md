# Slides and Visuals — VIS

Slides are the last thing built and the first thing over-built. The governing physical fact: **an audience reads or listens, never both.** If the slide is readable prose, you are redundant while it is up. Everything in this module follows from that.

The prior question this module always asks first: *do you need slides at all?* No slides beats bad slides, and a large share of the best-received talks in existence use none.

---

## VIS-01 — What did you actually build?

**Trigger.** Before designing a single slide, and again before shipping.

**Procedure.**

1. Count the words on the densest slide. That tells you what artifact you have:

   | Words per slide | What it is | What to do |
   |---|---|---|
   | > 75 | a document | write a real document; do not project it |
   | ~50 | a teleprompter | you will read it aloud; cut to keywords |
   | few, plus a visual | a presentation | continue |

2. Keep the **three uses of slide software separate**, and build them as separate artifacts:
   - **documents** — circulated, portrait, paginated, PDF, never projected
   - **speaker notes** — in the notes pane, presenter view, never projected
   - **projected visuals** — images, diagrams, and short phrases that move the idea along
   Merging them produces the hybrid that does neither job.
3. Accept the cost: two artifacts instead of one. That is the only arrangement in which both work. A deck printed *as* the handout is a document that was projected, or a presentation that cannot be read.
4. Where the audience will read in advance, or wants to check your arithmetic — analysts, a board, a regulator — a written memo may persuade better than any deck. Choose it.

**Gates.**
- The artifact type is stated with how it will be used, and with the type rejected.
- Notes are in the notes pane, not on the slide.
- A separate handout exists if one is needed.

**Failure signals.**
- One file doing all three jobs.
- You are asked to "send the deck" and it is the same file you projected.
- Slides you would be lost without.
- Pride in slide count.

## VIS-02 — The gate every slide must pass

**Trigger.** Each slide, individually.

**Procedure.**

1. Each slide must do exactly one of three things, or be deleted:
   - **reveal** something hard to describe in words
   - **explain** something words cannot carry
   - **delight** — and earn it
2. **One idea per slide.** Slides are free; time is not. More slides beat denser slides. If two bullets do not need to be together, give each its own slide.
3. The **three-second rule**: a slide is glance media, closer to a billboard than a page. If it cannot be processed in about three seconds, it is not a slide yet. Split it or cut it.
4. The **title is a conclusion, not a topic.** "Q3 regional performance" is a topic. "Three regions carried the quarter; the fourth is the problem" is a conclusion. Replace every descriptive title with an assertion.
5. Never put the script on the slide. The audience reads faster than you speak, so they arrive at the end before you do and then wait, disengaged. Use text to open a question, not to answer it.
6. **Reveal progressively.** Show elements only as you speak to them, and blank to black when a visual's job is done. A visual left up after its moment competes with the next thing you say.
7. Cut anything you would have to explain. If the slide needs explaining, the explanation is the content and the slide is in the way.

**Gates.**
- Every slide reveals, explains, or delights.
- Every title asserts.
- Three-second rule passes on every slide.
- No script on any slide.

**Failure signals.**
- Two-line titles.
- Sub-sub-bullets.
- The audience reading ahead of you.
- Type consistently under 24 point with third-level bullets — you built a document.
- A logo on every slide, a bordered legend, clip art.
- "And here is a picture of…" — the speaker has become a technician narrating slides.

## VIS-03 — Construction

**Trigger.** Building or auditing the visual design.

**Procedure.**

1. Fix **arrangement** before touching colour or type. Slides fail from construction, not from style, and decorating a broken slide is the wrong repair.
   - Build on a grid drawn on the master and then hidden.
   - No more than three information layers on a plane; one main point plus at most two sub-points.
   - Leave tiles open. White space is as important as a pause in speech.
   - Watch for **unintentional contrast** — accidental differences in size, weight or proximity that emphasize the wrong thing.
   - Ask constantly: *what can I remove without changing the meaning?*
2. Everything on a slide is **signal or noise**. There is no third category.
3. **Strategic contrast**: the more things you make different, the less any of them stands out. Highlight at most about a tenth of the design.
4. Type: never below 28 point for a keynote presentation; two typefaces maximum, one is fine. Test legibility physically — read it from a distance in feet equal to your monitor's diagonal in inches, or view the deck at two-thirds size and delete anything unreadable.
5. Colour: three to five core colours plus one neutral and one highlight. The neutral pushes secondary material back; the highlight is the only look-here cue. Check in greyscale and for colour vision deficiency, and never use red for a positive number.
6. Background: dark for a formal large venue where objects should glow; light for an informal room, an uncontrolled projector, or a deck that will be printed. Never midtones or pastels — projectors blow them out.
7. Depth: pick flat **or** dimensional and commit. One light source, one horizon, one vanishing point, applied to everything. Mixing them produces a subliminal distrust the audience cannot name.
8. **Diagrams are selected, not invented.** Name the relationship in words first, then choose the family that fits it: flow (a process), structure (a classification or hierarchy), cluster (grouping and overlap), radiate (links to a core), influence (forces acting on something), or a realistic depiction. If the relationship will not fit a family, rename the relationship — do not bend the diagram.
9. Convert bullet slides into diagrams whenever the items are actually related. Circle the nouns and verbs, name the relationship, and replace the list.
10. Decompose anything too complex for one diagram into an ordered series unified by a single motif, built up over time. The audience processes one idea at a time regardless of how complete your architecture is.
11. Animation must serve exactly one of five purposes — a change in relationship, a change in direction, a change in the object, a sequence, or emphasis. Delete every effect that serves none. Left-to-right movement reads as protagonist and is the easiest to follow; right-to-left ascending is the hardest.
12. Use images as a **system**, as though one photographer shot them all: matched subject, lighting and tone. No clip art, no grabbed web images, no stock metaphors.

**Gates.**
- Arrangement fixed before colour and type.
- ≤ 3 information layers per slide; ≤ 10% highlighted.
- Every diagram matched to a named relationship.
- Every animation matched to one of the five purposes.

**Failure signals.**
- A slide fixed by adding colour.
- Mixed flat and dimensional elements.
- An animated build with no communicative purpose.
- Every slide with a different visual idiom.

## VIS-04 — Slides for a technical or architectural subject

**Trigger.** The content includes systems, architectures, models, pipelines or platforms, and the audience is not technical.

**Procedure.**

1. Do not put the architecture diagram on the wall and narrate it. An architecture slide answers a question a non-technical audience is not asking, and it converts you from a leader into a technician in one click.
2. Show, in this order: the **business problem**, the **current friction** (what a person does today, and how long it takes), the **decision** a human makes in that flow, where the capability enters, what the human still controls, the **boundary** it must not cross, the expected outcome, and how it will be measured. That is the sequence, and each element is a slide or part of one.
3. Explain the mechanism at the level the audience needs to make the decision, and no deeper. Do not overload — and do not turn the technology into magic either. An audience that cannot form any model of how it works cannot own the decision, and will delegate it back to you.
4. **Visualize the uncertainty and the limits.** A slide that shows only the success case reads as a sales pitch to exactly the audience you need to trust you. Show the failure mode, the confidence range, or the case where it does not apply.
5. Use real data. A fabricated example is detected instantly by anyone in the room who knows the domain, and their scepticism then attaches to everything else.
6. Every technical slide connects to a business decision on the same slide, or it does not go in.
7. Include an **action slide**: the specific commitments, owners and dates. See `ACT-01`.

**Gates.**
- No architecture diagram used as a narration prop.
- Business problem before mechanism, always.
- Limits and uncertainty shown, not only the success path.
- Every technical slide tied to a decision.

**Failure signals.**
- Boxes and arrows with the speaker tracing them.
- A capability presented with no boundary.
- Model or vendor names doing the work a result should do.
- An entirely green forecast.

## Defaults and thresholds

| Decision | Default |
|---|---|
| Ideas per slide | 1 |
| Slide comprehension | ≤ 3 seconds |
| Slide dwell time | ≤ 2 minutes |
| Minimum type | 28 pt keynote; never below 24 |
| Typefaces / colours | 1–2 typefaces; 3–5 colours + 1 neutral + 1 highlight |
| Highlighted share of a design | ≤ ~10% |
| Information layers per slide | ≤ 3 |
| Slide rate, rough | ~1 per 2 minutes, but count is not the metric |
| Content built for | ~60% of the slot |
| Deck trim when short of time | cut a third into a cut file, not into the appendix |
| Executive summary length | ~10% of the appendix it summarizes |
| Handout | separate artifact, distributed at the end |
