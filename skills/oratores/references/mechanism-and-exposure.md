# Mechanism and Exposure — MEC

Two capabilities, one module.

**Forward:** which mechanism is doing the work in your piece, and what the trade-off is — because the same content built on a different mechanism produces a different result.

**Backward:** reading someone else's message the same way. The inventory below is the full one, and it is at least as useful pointed outward as pointed forward.

**A note on the form.** Each entry states *when to use it* and *what it costs*. Where a cost is stated, it is a documented one. Where a mechanism has no documented cost, none is invented to fill the gap — the line is simply absent, and an absent line means unknown, not free.

**What this module does not do.** It does not decide which mechanism you take. That depends on context the package does not have — a message whose whole objective is speed of conviction in an emergency is a different job from one that has to leave an organization able to think in two years. Both are real. The costs are stated so they can be weighed.

---

## MEC-01 — Mechanism selection

**Trigger.** The work is being done by something other than the case itself — which is most of the time.

**Procedure.**

1. **Name the mechanism, in writing, before you use it.** A mechanism you cannot name is one you cannot tune, cannot tell is misfiring, and cannot answer for when asked.
2. **Offer the alternatives rather than silently picking one.** Where two mechanisms would both work and cost differently, state both with their trade-offs and let the author choose. One version handed over with the alternative invisible makes a choice that was not yours to make.
3. Read the trade-off before committing.

### Mechanisms, and what each costs

**A case or reference from the audience's own context.**
*Use when* the audience will not move on a general claim. The pragmatic majority does not buy without references from inside its own industry, and early adopters are structurally poor references for them — the two want different things from the same product.
*Costs:* you need the reference to exist. There is no substitute at this stage, which is why one contained, complete, referenceable win matters more than several partial ones.

**A conceded weakness, volunteered before the claim.**
*Use when* you must be believed on something that serves you.
*Costs:* the weakness must be genuine and secondary. A manufactured one is detected. Related: naming your own limitation once is the cheapest credibility available; asserting your own honesty is evidence against it.

**A demonstration.**
*Use when* the audience needs to see that the thing exists at all.
*Costs:* a demonstration convinces the enthusiast and the visionary; the pragmatist wants market share, references, and the number and commitment of partners. Do not expect a demo to carry an audience it was never going to convince.

**Social proof.**
*Use when* the audience lacks expertise, the situation is ambiguous, or the decision is an adoption decision.
*Costs:* strongest under uncertainty and similarity — and it *collapses, deservedly, when the proof is transparently faked.* Publicizing a minority norm backfires: telling people that few comply tells them what normal is.

**Authority.**
*Use when* a claim needs credibility fast.
*Costs:* the symbols are trivially counterfeited, which is why the mechanism works better than it should and why verification matters more than it feels like it should. Audit yours the way a sceptic will: truly an expert, *relevant* expert, and what do they gain?

**Scarcity or a deadline.**
*Use when* you need a decision now rather than understanding.
*Costs:* the arousal that makes it work is the same arousal that suppresses deliberation — in the audience and in you. Amplified by newly-imposed scarcity and by visible rivalry. And **fabricated deadlines destroy trust permanently once detected.**

**Fear.**
*Use when* there is one specific, well-understood action available immediately.
*Costs:* **fear alone produces avoidance of the message; fear plus a feasible action produces compliance.** This is a finding about what works, not a rule about what is permitted — fear without a way out does not fail because it is unfair, it fails because the audience defends itself against the message instead of acting on it. Ship the first without the second and you have chosen the version that does not work. Where the problem is ambiguous and needs invention rather than execution, fear is the wrong instrument: it narrows attention onto removing the threat, which is what makes change impossible rather than urgent.

**An enemy, or an attack on a group.**
*Use when* you need cohesion and a simplified choice.
*Costs:* two, from different sources and pointing the same way. Attacking a group **draws retaliation and can be self-fulfilling.** And moralising a division produces **non-negotiable lines of division, obscures the political nature of the issue, and forecloses transformation** — the sides outlive the campaign that named them. Separately: mere contact between hostile groups worsens things; what reduces hostility is interdependent common goals, not exhortation.

**Identity** ("what would someone like me do here").
*Use when* self-interest is thin, absent, or insulting.
*Costs:* if a change violates an identity, **incentives will fail** — sell the identity instead. Identities can be adopted and even invented, but never through deception. The stated test is whether you could say your strategy out loud in the room without embarrassment.

**A commitment the audience makes themselves.**
*Use when* the behaviour must persist without supervision.
*Costs:* it must be active, public, effortful and freely chosen. **Large rewards or threats destroy the effect** — they buy compliance and not belief, and the compliance stops when the pressure does.

**A story.**
*Use when* the audience needs to know how to act, not only what is true.
*Costs:* a story is recalled where an isolated statistic is not, by a wide margin — and a story shipped without a resolution produces paralysis rather than action. A great story carrying the wrong plot pushes the wrong behaviour.

**A metaphor or frame.**
*Use when* you need a whole response set adopted at once rather than a single fact.
*Costs:* metaphors **smuggle in commitments you may not want** — verify the entailment set. A powerful one can be turned against you by a rival in a single line, and the more memorable it is, the likelier that is.

**A number with a comparator.**
*Use when* scale is the argument.
*Costs:* **the comparison choice is where dishonesty lives.** Use a statistic as input to your judgement, not as output shopped after the conclusion. And adding aggregate statistics to a personal appeal measurably weakens it — never prime calculation before an appeal.

**Repetition.**
*Use when* a claim must become familiar, or a list must become a refrain.
*Costs:* the surprise effect wears off and the audience becomes **both immune and addicted**. Density of devices reads as spin — if every paragraph contains one, the piece reads as all technique and no substance.

**Simplification of a complex situation.**
*Use when* the facts are too intricate for the audience to hold, or the simple explanation is genuinely the most plausible.
*Costs:* **the most frequently used and most powerful pattern available, and the characteristic engine of conspiracy theories.** Neutral with respect to framing, and usable to build one.

**Inoculation** — raising the objection yourself, first.
*Use when* the audience will hear the other side.
*Costs:* **over-inoculating can plant doubts the audience did not hold.**

**Held attention** — mystery, an open loop, self-relevance.
*Use when* the case is strong but requires attention to unglamorous detail.
*Costs:* magnetisers deliver attention, not assent. They **amplify whatever follows, including a weak case, against you.** Only pin attention when the case is genuinely strong.

### Three cross-cutting frames

These three carry more weight than the individual entries above, because each is a documented regularity rather than a judgement about one mechanism.

**Durability** — how long an effect survives unattended:

| What you used | It holds for | To extend it |
|---|---|---|
| A one-off prime, nothing else | minutes to hours | add a commitment |
| A prime plus an expressed judgement | until attention shifts | seal it in writing |
| An active, chosen, effortful commitment | months | — |
| A cue at a recurring decision point | indefinite; it re-fires | keep the cue in place |
| The same claim repeated across occasions | slow to take, then very lasting | keep the wording identical |

Never rely on a prime for anything you need next week. Convert it to a commitment or an environmental cue. See `ACT-01`.

**Phase** — a mechanism fired in the wrong phase is the commonest reason a technically correct move reads as pressure:

| Phase | Goal | Pre-activate | Never yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Cultivate | be accepted | reciprocity, liking, unity | scarcity |
| 2. Reduce uncertainty | be believed | social proof, authority | closing pressure |
| 3. Motivate action | be acted on | consistency, scarcity | new rapport-building |

If the ask is not landing, diagnose the **phase**, not the argument. Scarcity before trust is pressure; rapport at closing time is stalling.

**Agitation or integration** — the primary division in the propaganda literature, and the one that carries the time horizon:

| | Agitation | Integration |
|---|---|---|
| Aim | break something loose — rebellion, sacrifice, hatred of an enemy | make people fit — conformity, adherence, adjustment |
| Cost | cheap, fast | expensive, slow; needs mass media and an affluent audience |
| Relation to truth | indifferent to it | must *look* informative |
| Works best on | the poorer and less informed | **the comfortable and educated** — counter-intuitively, the harder audience for one is the easier for the other |

Two further findings from the same literature, stated as trade-offs rather than as rules:

- **The effect ceiling is low, and the literature does not agree on how low.** One tradition treats these mechanisms as powerful and cumulative. The other, working from a century of effect studies, reports no catalogue of techniques that reliably work: **reinforcement is the ordinary outcome and change is the exception**, requiring several things to converge at once — the channel, the social context, the group, the opinion leaders, and a source the audience already credits. Both are in this corpus. The practical consequence either way: expect to harden what is already there, treat a genuine change of position as the expensive case, and do not read a list of mechanisms as a list of levers that fire on demand.
- **Propaganda can ride, focus and intensify a society's deep tendencies; it cannot create or reverse them.** A campaign riding one looks omnipotent while doing almost no work. One pushing against a fundamental current fails whatever its budget. Establish which you are doing before crediting or blaming the campaign — including your own.
- **Content-indifference is not immunity.** An audience that has stopped caring about the content stays sensitized to the stimulus and keeps obeying the reflex. It is anaesthetized to the argument and wired to the cue, so the dose that fires it gets **smaller** over time rather than larger; the signature is declining attention alongside continued compliance. Which is also why reading a saturated room as resistant and answering it with more force mistakes the mechanism. Where the objective is a decision the audience owns rather than a reflex it obeys, a stronger dose confirms the fatigue instead of overcoming it — see `EXE-02`, which is that case.

Where the objective genuinely is speed of conviction — an emergency, an evacuation, an outbreak — one version of the agitation profile matches the job: hammer a single salient threat and attach a feasible action to it. That is the version the analytic literature describes, and it is what the fear entry above already requires. It is **not** the version in the table, which is built on hatred of a designated enemy and works best on the poorer and less informed. Before taking that one, check both preconditions: that this is the audience it works on, and that the enemy is the thing you meant to name. Either way the costs above are being traded deliberately against an immediate objective. The literature states the trade; it does not make it, and neither does this module.

4. **Distinguish a real cue from a fabricated one.** The source position on this is blunt and it is stated as a systems argument rather than a moral one: an honest cue is a cooperating partner, and a fabricated one steals the reliability of the shortcut itself — which is the resource everyone, including you, needs in order to function. Operationally: a real limit, a real peer, a real credential or a real case survives being noticed. A fabricated one is a one-use asset with an unbounded liability, and the same literature notes that once detected the damage is permanent rather than repairable.
5. Note where the effect comes from **conditions** rather than from the message — time pressure, crowding, fatigue, overload. Correction requires the audience to notice the frame, know its direction, and have both the motivation and the spare capacity to adjust; pressure disables all three, which is why pressure environments get engineered. Two practical consequences: the effect ends when the conditions do, and it is visible to anyone reviewing how the meeting was run.
6. Where the mechanism carries weight the case cannot, say so in your notes. That is a design finding — usually the case needs strengthening or the ask needs shrinking — not a verdict.

**Gates.**
- The mechanism is named.
- Its stated trade-off is on the table before the choice is made.
- Where a real alternative exists, it is offered with its different cost.
- Phase and horizon are both matched deliberately.

**Failure signals.**
- Scarcity or a deadline before trust exists.
- Rapport-building at closing time.
- A prime relied on for a decision that will be taken next month.
- An enemy named in a piece whose objective needs those two groups working together afterwards.
- Unity or kinship language with no shared genealogy, place or joint action behind it — this is similarity dressed as unity; it moves nothing and it is detected.
- One version delivered where two genuinely different options existed.

## MEC-02 — Speaking for a group

**Trigger.** The piece claims to represent a group — an organization, a profession, a workforce, a market, a public.

**Procedure.**

1. Start from the structural fact, because it sets how much weight the claim carries: **no claimed group includes every one of its members.** Any assertion of speaking for the whole is in principle inaccurate, including when it is innocuous and including when the cause is good. That is a measure of load-bearing capacity, not an accusation.
2. Fragility scales with the share actually represented. "Speaking for the delivery teams" is durable. "Speaking for everyone in this firm" is contestable by any one person present, and the contest is usually public.
3. Watch how the draft handles the people it does not speak for. This scale is from the populism literature, where it is used as a risk gradient — movement *up* it is the signal, not the presence of the claim itself:

   | Position | What the piece does with dissent | What it costs |
   |---|---|---|
   | 1 | ignores it | nothing, until someone raises it |
   | 2 | acknowledges it and keeps speaking for everyone | the contradiction is visible but tolerated |
   | 3 | treats dissenters as deceived or under-informed | — |
   | 4 | treats dissent as conspiracy, self-interest or obstruction | — |
   | 5 | denies dissenters membership — not real professionals, not committed | exclusion from the group itself; at its limit, scapegoating and persecution |

   The scale is a risk gradient and the literature prices only its top. Positions 3 and 4 carry no stated cost, which means unpriced rather than free. What *is* stated is the direction of travel: each step moves the claim further from the case and closer to the dissenter's motive, and each is easier to take than to walk back.

4. Check the language pass for the quiet version — a definite plural presupposing unanimity. "The firm has decided." "Our people want." "The market has moved." Each presupposes a totality that does not exist; it is usually a drafting habit rather than an intention, and it is the version most easily contradicted, because one counterexample does it.
5. Where you flatter a group, notice who is outside it. A complimentary adjective before a group noun tells everyone excluded what they are — sometimes exactly what you want, and it should be a decision.
6. Splitting a group into good members and bad leaders in order to claim the good half is a documented and effective move. Two documented costs, neither cosmetic. The division it draws is moral rather than practical, which is what makes it hard to undo: you cannot later ask the two halves to compromise over something you framed as virtue against betrayal. And a vertical split between a people and its leaders reliably acquires a horizontal one between insiders and outsiders — those excluded by the flattering adjective hear it.
7. Where the ask is genuinely for a part — a function, a region, a seniority band — say so. Speaking openly for a part is not the problem; claiming to speak for the whole is what gets contested.

**Gates.**
- Any claim to represent a group is either accurate or narrowed.
- The position on the dissent scale is written down, with the position below it that was rejected and why.
- Presupposed unanimity flagged in the language pass.

**Failure signals.**
- "We all know", "everyone agrees", "nobody thinks".
- A segment characterized as behind or not yet understanding — see `AUD-02`; their objection usually contains the real obstacle.
- A claim to speak for a workforce that was not asked, in a room containing that workforce.

## MEC-03 — Exposure management

**Trigger.** The piece is attributable, recorded, quotable, regulated, or will be read by anyone with an interest in checking it. In practice: nearly always.

This procedure is about **what gets caught and what it costs**, not about what is permissible.

**Procedure.**

1. Decide before drafting what standard of evidence the piece holds to. A decision taken under deadline with a stakeholder in the room is not a decision.
2. Apply both `LOG-04` axes to every material claim — the type, and the status of the source. The four failures below are all failures of type; they all survive fact-checking and all detonate later:
   - a **forecast** in the grammar of a fact
   - a **pilot** described as a rollout
   - a **capability** described as a practice
   - a **demonstration** described as a proof
   A senior audience has seen all four conflated before, which is much of why it discounts the next claim. See `EXE-02`.
3. Where the number does not exist, say so. An invented benchmark or an anonymous "research shows" is the fastest way to lose a room containing one informed person, and a room of five hundred contains one.
4. When the supportable version is weaker, the options are: find better evidence; narrow the claim; state it with its uncertainty attached; or drop it.
5. The high-frequency failure is not a false statement; it is **misleading by omission and construction** — leaving out what you do not trust rather than flagging it, stripping context that would change the reading, choosing the comparison after the conclusion, presenting the best case as the expected one. Put the fact-check in the process rather than relying on care.
6. Where you are instructed to make a claim the evidence does not support: say once, plainly, in the brief, what the evidence does support. Then write the piece as decided, removing the unsupported claim rather than softening it — a softened false claim is still false and now carries your judgement.
7. Where something delivered turns out to be wrong: **correct the record and do not repeat it.** Once, explicitly. The uncorrected version is what compounds.
8. Keep the receipts. Every number, quotation and attribution should be traceable to something producible within a day.

**Gates.**
- Evidence standard set before drafting.
- Every claim carries a type and a source status, and both are correct.
- No invented figure anywhere.
- Every number and quotation traceable.

**Failure signals.**
- "Studies show", "industry benchmarks", "we've seen returns of" with nothing behind them.
- A best case presented as an expectation.
- A comparison chosen after the conclusion.
- A caveat in the notes and absent from the slide.
- A correction that never happened because nobody asked.

## MEC-04 — Reading someone else's persuasion

**Trigger.** Assessing a message, a campaign, a competitor's pitch, or an information environment. Also useful on your own draft, run cold.

**Procedure.**

1. **Classify before judging.** Four axes; the combination predicts behaviour better than any single label:
   - **authored or diffuse** — a deliberate campaign with a source, versus something rising out of a whole way of working with no author
   - **agitation or integration** — see the table in `MEC-01`
   - **vertical or horizontal** — from a centre through channels, versus produced inside the group by peers. **Horizontal runs deeper and is far harder to reverse.**
   - **rational in form or not** — factual and technical in *presentation* while seeking a non-rational adherence. Modern material is nearly always the first while being the second
2. **Separate the facts from the intention and the interpretation.** Verify the facts — modern material usually *wins* on facts, and that is the point. Then audit what was omitted, what context was stripped, and what action the fact licenses.
3. **Read for what is asked, not what is asserted.** What *action* is wanted? Would performing it produce the belief? Where a message ends in participation, the conviction is the by-product.
4. Check the **existing current** — see `MEC-01`. Riding one, focusing one, or opposing one predicts more than the campaign's quality does.
5. Trace the **preparation.** Where a message succeeds implausibly fast, or a slogan needs no explanation, the conditioning ran earlier. A short campaign with large effects is evidence of long preparation, not of brilliance.
6. Identify the source: who gains if the audience complies; who speaks most and most authoritatively; whether the visible sponsor is the actual one. Watch the two laundering shapes — a source **created** by the interested party and credited by the audience, and a message **placed** in a credible outlet then quoted back from it. The original placement is the evidentiary chokepoint.
7. Judge effect **behaviourally**: joining, funding, adopting, demonstrating, changing a process, using the sender's vocabulary. Discount sender-supplied figures, which are routinely inflated. Do not read commentary or a published poll as the target audience's reaction.
8. Then: **would this outcome have occurred without it?**
9. Report what you could not answer as a gap. An unanswerable division is a finding, not a failure.
10. Apply the same standard to every side, including your own. Non-partisanship is the source of this kind of analysis's credibility, and abandoning it is how the work loses its value.

**Gates.**
- Classified on all four axes before judgement.
- Facts separated from intention and interpretation.
- Source identified, including the laundering check.
- Effect judged behaviourally.
- Gaps reported as gaps.

**Failure signals.**
- "It's only propaganda if it's lies."
- "Education will inoculate people" — the literature runs the other way: literacy plus an information glut without critical capacity is a *precondition*, and the well-informed absorb the most unverifiable second-hand material.
- "I'm too informed to be affected."
- Effect measured by opinion survey where the aim was behaviour.
- One side analyzed.

## MEC-05 — Recognition catalogue

**Trigger.** Reviewing any message, yours or someone else's. These are the moves that work — which is exactly why they need to be recognizable on sight.

| Tell | What it usually is |
|---|---|
| A spontaneous-looking event with a named committee, prominent patrons and a convenient date | the event was built so the conclusion would be *observed* rather than argued |
| An apparently disinterested body — institute, bureau, benchmark, panel — funded by an interested party | borrowed independence; check the funding trail |
| A story arriving via a convenient credible third party | the claim was placed there so it could be quoted back |
| Free, broadcast-ready material arriving at no cost | the source is undisclosed by design |
| Goodwill with no ask — grants, exchanges, sponsored research, hospitality | preparation, banked against a later request |
| A renaming after which the criticism evaporates and nothing else changed | the practice was untouched; only the expectation moved |
| Small tasks solicited before any commitment is requested | the task is expected to produce the commitment |
| A euphemism that inverts its referent | the frame doing what the argument cannot |
| An enemy named, personified and morally simplified | outrage substituting for a case — and a division that outlasts the campaign |
| An outrageous charge rather than a measured one | exaggeration chosen because it evokes more belief than a proportionate claim |
| An accusation with no refutable claim in it | innuendo |
| A venue too small for the crowd | a groundswell being manufactured |
| A speaker before a very large image of themselves or their institution | scale doing the work of standing |
| An identical message across every channel and level | central authority enforcing homogeneity — not the same as agreement |
| An audience adopting the sender's slogans, vocabulary and dress | a real behavioural effect, and a new symbolic identity |
| A tidy, economical explanation of something complicated | simplification tipping toward conspiracy |
| Ambiguous scandalous phrasing followed by indignant denial | both the transgression and the martyrdom, harvested |
| Positions that reverse quickly and in both directions | discord blurred to preserve an image of unanimity |
| "The truth about…", "what they aren't telling you" | the outsider-truth-teller pose; expect revelation to substitute for evidence |

Two notes. First, several of these have entirely legitimate versions — a real event, a genuinely independent institute, a renaming that reflects a real change. A tell is a prompt to check, not a verdict. Second, the higher-value application is on your own drafts, where you will find two or three you did not intend.

**Procedure.**

1. Run the table over the artifact once, forward, marking every row that fires. Do not stop at the first.
2. For each mark, check it rather than scoring it: the legitimate version and the engineered version look identical in the artifact and differ in the world. Name what would distinguish them — the funder, the date, the person who chose the comparator, whether the renaming tracked a real change.
3. Where the check is available, run it and record the answer. Where it is not, record the tell as **unresolved** and say what would resolve it. An unresolved tell is not a finding.
4. Run the same pass over your own draft last, when you are least inclined to. That is where the two or three you did not intend are.

**Gates.**
- The catalogue was applied to the whole artifact, not sampled.
- Every tell that fired is either checked, or recorded as unresolved with the check named.
- No tell reported as a verdict on the strength of the tell alone.
- The pass was run on your own draft as well as on theirs.

**Failure signals.**
- A tell listed as a finding with no check attempted.
- The catalogue run only outward.
- A legitimate version called out because it matched the shape.
- A clean report on a piece whose numbers were never traced.
