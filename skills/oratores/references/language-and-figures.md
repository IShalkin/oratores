# Language and Figures — LNG

Writing the actual sentences. The governing constraint is physical: an article is written for the eye, a speech for the ear, and **the ear is a far coarser instrument**. Build a spoken piece like a caricature — bold lines, few strokes, deliberate repetition — not like a photograph.

The corollary most drafts violate: the reason to speak rather than send the text is the second stream of information that turns information into conviction. If the words only work on the page, send the page.

---

## LNG-01 — Plain style

**Trigger.** Every draft, at word level.

**Procedure.**

1. Short words. Concrete words. Active verbs. One idea per sentence. Few adjectives. Contractions.
2. Make your words **smaller than your ideas**. Long words and long sentences signal someone with something to prove; readers reliably attribute the brief message to the senior person and the convoluted one to the junior.
3. Replace concept nouns with what actually happened. "Received a standing ovation" → "stood up, clapping, shouting". "Suboptimal engagement" → "half of them stopped opening it".
4. Run the passive-voice scan on every finished draft. The passive is the exculpatory voice; the active is the accountable one. "The policy will be implemented" versus "we're doing it on Monday". Where the passive is the right choice — to veil an actor deliberately — make that a decision, not a habit.
5. Kill the manufactured verbs (`-ize`), the formal connectives (therefore → so; however → but; nevertheless → still; moreover → what's more), and the throat-clearing openers ("it is necessary to", "it should be noted that").
6. Replace institutional pronouns with human ones: "we" and "you", not "management" and "employees". Ration the first-person singular; reserve it for personal responsibility.
7. Punctuate for the mouth. A draft with no dashes and no ellipses is an article. Semicolons are a sign it was written for the eye.
8. Reject the three myths: that sentences must always be short, the voice always active, and the grammar always perfect. Choose sentence length **for effect** — clipped and connective-free for urgency, long and rolling for calm and command — and prefer authenticity to correctness when they conflict.

**Gates.**
- Passive scan run.
- No load-bearing abstraction left unconverted.
- Sentence length varies deliberately across the piece.
- Every institutional noun replaced or justified.

**Failure signals.**
- Jargon clusters: deliverables, frameworks, levers, beacons, workstreams, north stars.
- "Certain errors in judgement were made."
- Semicolons in a spoken text.
- Every sentence the same length — the fastest route to a room stopping listening.

## LNG-02 — Figures, by function

**Trigger.** Drafting the lines that must carry rhythm, emphasis, or quotation.

**Procedure.** Choose the device by the job, not by taste.

**To manufacture the appearance of logic:**

| Job | Device |
|---|---|
| Frame a choice; dramatize a gap | **antithesis** — set the idea beside its opposite, tightly and symmetrically, then resolve it |
| Make a claim sound complete | **rule of three** — exactly three, grammatically parallel, strongest last |
| Sound balanced (and therefore be believed to be) | pair every assertion with its contrast or comparison; never assert alone |
| Make a line aphoristic | **reversal** — "planned to fail / failed to plan" |
| Make a claim believable | **rhyme** — people believe rhyming claims more, and deny being influenced. Spend it on the one line that cannot miss |
| Bind a sequence | **alliteration**, sparingly |

**To carry feeling:**

| Job | Device |
|---|---|
| Speaker as the one acting | **anaphora** — repeat the opening words across successive units |
| Speaker as the one acted upon | **epistrophe** — repeat the closing words |
| Urgency, agitation | strip the connectives; short sentences |
| Calm, command, time in hand | restore the connectives; long periodic sentences |
| Make the audience *see* it | describe the thing **in action**, and give inanimate things agency |
| Make the audience lean in | withhold the identifying fact to the final clause, with a marked pause |

**To carry character:**

Plain monosyllabic truth. A deeply personal statement. A general maxim stated aloud that the audience already holds — this is what makes a piece sound like a person rather than a document, and it rings false the moment it exceeds the speaker's actual experience.

Then:

1. **Ration them.** Every paragraph containing a device reads as all spin and no substance. Weight and feeling run throughout; ornament appears at intervals.
2. Use a list of more than three items as **anaphora**, not as a list. Any list can be converted, and the conversion is what makes it quotable, emotional, and a transition all at once. A list collapses all three.
3. Order any list **climactically**, and vary the last item like a punchline.
4. Where you need one line to be remembered, coin exactly **one**, and build it with one device, not five: contrast, rhyme, reversal, alliteration, or metaphor. Two memorable lines cancel; the audience keeps one.
5. Set the line up: a short announcing clause in a different register, then a pause, then the line. Once per piece. Place it at the **end** of a thought, never before an explanation. Note the real counter-position: some speakers refuse the device as pompous and trade quotability for credibility deliberately. That is a legitimate choice — make it knowingly.

**Gates.**
- Every device chosen against a named job.
- Exactly one coined line, with one setup.
- Lists of more than three converted to anaphora.
- Device density inside the stated threshold: ornament at intervals, never a device in every sentence of a paragraph.
- Any device taken from the appearance-of-logic table has run `PTH-05`'s stated-out-loud test.

**Failure signals.**
- Four items in a triad, or two.
- Alliteration you notice.
- A device in every paragraph.
- A memorable line placed before the explanation of it.

## LNG-03 — Metaphor

**Trigger.** Once per piece, as the central image; and any time a concept resists explanation.

**Procedure.**

1. Identify the substitution you are making — *x is y* — and check what else comes with `y`. A metaphor imports a whole response set, not a single comparison. Verify the entailments you did not intend.
2. Choose the source domain for its **connotation**:

   | Source | Implies | Use for |
   |---|---|---|
   | Nature, weather, seasons | inevitability | something you want treated as coming regardless |
   | Journey, road, distance | momentum, effort, a destination | a programme with stages |
   | Family, friendship, neighbourhood | safety, belonging | trust, retention, mutual obligation |
   | Food, sustenance, growth | nourishment, patience | capability building |
   | Craft, art, instrument | freedom, skill, mastery | professional identity |
   | Illness and cure | urgency, blame, a diagnosis | a failure you want treated as pathological |
   | War, enemies, campaigns | conflict, sides, sacrifice | rarely; it creates the sides it names |
   | Machine, engine, parts | uniformity, replaceability | almost never — it flatters the leader and reduces the audience to components |

3. Prefer metaphors drawn from the same class as the thing, and beautiful or dignified in proportion to the effect wanted. You can raise or lower the same act simply by choosing the higher or lower term for it — that is the whole of praise and blame, and it is worth noticing when it is done to you.
4. Prefer **analogy** (A is to B as C is to D) and prefer images that address the eye. A metaphor drawn from too far off is a riddle; from too near, it teaches nothing.
5. Test by extension: take the comparison seriously and run it one step further. If the extension is absurd, the metaphor is a conceit and will chill the passage rather than warm it.
6. **Mirror the audience's own metaphors** and answer inside them. Answering with your own marks you as a different tribe.
7. Beware the reversible image. Any vivid metaphor can be seized and turned in one line by a rival, and the more memorable it is the more likely that is.
8. Never dehumanize. Depersonalizing language about people — vermin, pests, dead weight, headcount — is how atrocity is prepared, and it is also how an organization loses the people it needed.

**Gates.**
- The entailment set is checked.
- The source domain is chosen for connotation, not convenience.
- The extension test is run.
- No dehumanizing image anywhere.

**Failure signals.**
- Two images in one sentence — mixed metaphor; and in a document it usually means either drafting by committee or deliberate obfuscation.
- A dead metaphor doing load-bearing work.
- Handshakes over globes, rockets, chess pieces, icebergs.
- An image a hostile reader could flip in one sentence.

## LNG-04 — Writing for the ear

**Trigger.** Any text that will be spoken.

**Procedure.**

1. Read every draft aloud. Not silently — aloud. This is the only test that catches most of what is wrong with a spoken text.
2. The **breath test**: say the sentence in one breath. If you cannot, it is too long, regardless of how it reads.
3. Retype the final text as phrases, one per line: break at each comma, never split a subject from its verb or a preposition from its object, never end a line on "a" or "the", stop at each full stop. This makes the eye-up delivery nearly automatic and exposes exactly where the rhythm is missing.
4. Prose should have **rhythm but not metre**. Unrhythmical prose is unpleasant; metrical prose is distracting and sounds contrived. The default sentence shape for anything to be heard is a unit with a beginning and an end in itself, of a length the listener can take in at once — neither so short that it trips them nor so long that they lose the thread.
5. Reserve the strongest phrase for the **final position** of the sentence. The end of a sentence is the point of greatest emphasis; a correct but limp final clause wastes it.
6. Test the sound as well as the sense: soft consonants for warmth and reassurance, hard consonants for force and decision. Where two words carry the same meaning, take the one that sounds like what you mean.
7. Write **transitions** as punctuation for the ear: a word, a repeated phrase, a question, a number, or a pause. Short pause is a comma, longer is a full stop, longest is a section break. Write PAUSE into the text where it matters.
8. Mark the text before delivery: two or three key words per sentence, one word per paragraph, the lightest sentence, the biggest turn. Rehearse the marks, then rehearse the feeling.

**Gates.**
- Read aloud at least once, end to end.
- No sentence fails the breath test.
- Final text laid out in phrases.
- Transitions written, not assumed.

**Failure signals.**
- A sentence you have to restart.
- The strongest word in the middle of the sentence.
- Statements that rise in pitch at the end — this costs authority, and it is usually a punctuation problem in the text.
- A page break mid-sentence in the lectern copy.

## LNG-05 — Quotation and borrowed language

**Trigger.** Any borrowed authority, wording, or idea.

**Procedure.**

1. One quotation per piece, staged. Two cancel each other.
2. Beyond that: the name must be recognizable **and** the quotation brief. If the source is obscure, either frame and stage it deliberately — produce it physically, name why you kept it — or cut it.
3. The **opponent's or critic's own words in your favour** carry more credibility than any other quotation type. Collect them in advance.
4. Never quote an author you are uncomfortable with, and never quote to decorate. Apply two tests: is it familiar enough to land, and fresh enough to be worth saying?
5. Mark the boundaries with a pause and a change of tone. Never say "and I quote", "quote unquote", or "I believe it was someone who said".
6. Reuse of an **idea** via substantial paraphrase is normal practice. Reuse of **wording** requires credit — a distinctive phrase counts, and the standard to apply is the academic one, not the convenient one. Attribution is cheap; make it in the sentence.
7. Never twist the piece to fit a quotation you like.

**Gates.**
- One quotation, recognizable and brief.
- Every borrowed phrase credited.
- No hedged attribution anywhere.

**Failure signals.**
- Three quotations in the first two minutes.
- A quotation whose author the audience does not know, used unstaged.
- A well-known line with the attribution guessed at.

## Defaults and thresholds

| Decision | Default |
|---|---|
| Triad elements | exactly 3 |
| Coined memorable lines | 1 per piece |
| Setup clauses for that line | 1 |
| Quotations | 1 per piece |
| Anaphora refrain spacing | roughly every half to full page |
| Sentence length, urgency | short, connective-free (~10–15 words) |
| Sentence length, calm/command | long and periodic |
| Sentence maximum | one breath |
| Sizing a script | ~120 words per written minute; delivered pace is slower |
| Device density | low enough that no paragraph reads as engineered |
