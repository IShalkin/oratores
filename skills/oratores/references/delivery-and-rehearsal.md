# Delivery and Rehearsal — DLV

Performance and preparation. The claim that organizes this module: a competent piece delivered well beats a brilliant piece delivered badly, and delivery is a **motor skill** — it improves with repetitions and not with understanding.

The single most consequential rule in the module, and the one most often broken: **never let words come out of your mouth while your eyes are looking down.** Reading and speaking at the same time breaks the circuit. Everything else here is easier than that and matters less.

---

## DLV-01 — Choosing and preparing the delivery mode

**Trigger.** Any piece that will be spoken.

**Procedure.**

1. Choose the mode honestly, against the time you will actually invest:

   | Mode | Gains | Costs | Choose when |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | Read from manuscript | exact wording, safety | eye contact and contact with the room, unless the technique below is drilled | the words are legally or politically load-bearing |
   | Read with the snapshot technique | exact wording **and** contact | a real motor skill; needs reps | high-stakes set-piece with an exact text |
   | Memorized verbatim | highest contact and precision | brittle, and a half-memorized talk is the one fatal choice | you can invest the hours, and you prove it |
   | Beats plus rehearsed transitions | freshness, resilience | less verbal precision | you know the material deeply |
   | Extemporaneous from a mastered plan | spontaneity with structure | needs disciplined preparation and surplus material | the default for most professional speaking |
   | Wholly impromptu | freshness | thin content, poor structure | never by choice |

   The one mode to avoid categorically is **half-memorized**. The audience hears the recital and the recall effort at once. If you cannot invest the time to memorize fully, use notes and be visibly using them — an honest note card costs nothing; concealed reading costs everything.
2. Always fully script and memorize the **opening** and the **close**. Master the middle as blocks of meaning rather than as sentences. That combination gives you a reliable start, a reliable finish, and room to move in between — and you always know where home is.
3. Drill the snapshot technique if you will read: look down and take in a phrase; bring the head **all the way** up; pause a full second; say it conversationally; then look down again. It feels jerky to you and conversational to them. Practise it against any prose on a makeshift lectern until it is automatic.
4. Lay the lectern copy out for the eye that must leave it: large serif type, generously spaced, phrases one per line, no italics, no hyphenation at a line end, never a page ending mid-sentence, pages numbered and not stapled, slid sideways rather than turned.
5. Mark the text before rehearsing: the two or three words per sentence that carry the new idea; the one word per paragraph; the lightest sentence; the biggest turn; where the pauses go. Rehearse the marks, then rehearse the feeling. If the first pass sounds contrived, redo it with more nuance rather than abandoning the marking.
6. Take the **least concealing support** you can be confident in. Where vulnerability and confidence conflict, choose confidence — but never fake eye contact while reading, which loses both.

**Gates.**
- Mode chosen against the hours actually available.
- Opening and close memorized.
- Snapshot technique drilled if reading.
- Text marked before rehearsal.

**Failure signals.**
- Flat tone with the eyes rising between paragraphs — half-memorized.
- Speaking with the head still coming up.
- Eyes drifting above the audience to a monitor.
- A lectern copy printed as ordinary prose.

## DLV-02 — Timing and the cut list

**Trigger.** Any timed slot. This is the most reliably useful procedure in the module.

**Procedure.**

1. Build for **less than the slot** — around nine-tenths at most, and 60% is a better default for anything with questions. The margin buys laughter, pauses, an interruption, a technical failure, and questions.
2. Time it **aloud**, not by word count, and time it more than once. Delivered pace is slower than reading pace, and slower again in a large room.
3. Build the **cut list before you need it**: rank sections by what you would drop first, and write the ranking down. On the day, cutting from a list takes seconds; cutting by judgement mid-talk destroys the structure.
4. Prepare **two endings** — a summary false ending and the real close. Running long, take the first.
5. Watch the position of your slot. First or second is safe. Later is a real cost, and last is a position to decline. If the programme is running late, cut; do not compress. Compressed delivery reads as panic and loses more than the missing content would have.
6. Recover time from the middle, never from the close.
7. Never signal that you are running short of time. See `OPN-03`.

**Gates.**
- Timed aloud at least twice.
- Written cut list, ranked.
- Two endings prepared.
- Content fits the slot with margin.

**Failure signals.**
- Landing within seconds of the allotted time — a sign of insecurity, not preparation.
- "I'll get through the last section quickly."
- Cutting the close because the middle ran long.
- Speeding up.

## DLV-03 — Voice, presence and the room

**Trigger.** Preparing the physical delivery. Include only the notes that differ from this speaker's default.

**Procedure.**

1. **Monotony is the cardinal fault.** Sameness in any element — pitch, pace, force, gesture, structure — kills attention regardless of content. Vary something deliberately every few sentences, and let the variation follow the meaning rather than a pattern; mechanical sing-song is worse than monotone.
2. Emphasize by **pausing, lowering and slowing** at least as often as by getting louder. To stress a word, pause before it; never shout it.
3. **The pause is the strongest instrument available.** Before an important phrase to create expectancy, after it to let it land, or both. A pause that feels exaggerated to you is usually about right. A pause filled with "um" or "so" is not a pause; it is a stumble — and the fix is silence, held confidently.
4. Pace: roughly conversational in an amplified room, and slower for a large hall or a formal set-piece. Deliberately slow delivery is the more common failure and reads as word-starvation. Get to a slower overall rate by **pausing between ideas**, not by drawling.
5. Take a deliberate pause before you begin: stand, look at individual people, and let the room settle. This is a psychological equalizer for anyone unknown, junior, quiet, or speaking in a second language, and it costs nothing.
6. Eye contact in units of thought: take in one idea, meet one person's eyes, deliver it to them, move to the next idea and the next person. About a second each — briefer looks furtive, longer discomfits. Pick three or four friendly faces spread around the room.
7. Gesture: fewer than instinct suggests, and more facial expression than instinct suggests. Move from the shoulder rather than the elbow; a gesture must be simultaneous with or slightly ahead of the words, never after. Assign **one** decisive physical act to the moment that matters rather than rehearsing a repertoire.
8. Stand grounded, or walk with intent and stop often. Move on a transition and stand still inside a point: movement during an argument competes with it, movement between arguments marks the seam.
9. Sentence length is a mood dial: short and connective-free manufactures urgency; long and flowing manufactures calm. Use it deliberately, and remember which one you want — urgency focuses a room on the threat, which is not the same as focusing it on the action.

**Gates.**
- At least one element varied deliberately and mapped to meaning.
- Pauses marked in the text.
- One assigned gesture, not a repertoire.
- Pace decided for this room.

**Failure signals.**
- Every sentence with the same melody.
- Rocking or shifting weight leg to leg.
- A gesture that gets an unintended laugh — it followed the words.
- Rising pitch at the end of statements.
- Notes read while the head is down.

## DLV-04 — Rehearsal and the review loop

**Trigger.** Any piece whose outcome matters.

**Procedure.**

1. **"It sounds rehearsed" means under-rehearsed.** Early passes edit the content, middle passes fix the timing, late passes polish the transitions. Most speakers stop after the first kind.
2. Rehearse from the **full text**, not from the notes, even where you will deliver from notes. The full text is where you discover which sentences do not work in the mouth.
3. **Record it and watch it.** This is unpleasant and is the highest-return step available. Correct **one** thing per pass; a list of seven corrections produces none.
4. Screen it with honest critics — people profiled like the target audience, including at least one deliberate sceptic. Give them the material in advance, run it through once, then go back section by section. Budget several times the length of the talk for the session, and take the feedback without defending.
5. Prefer critics from **outside** the immediate organization where you can. Internal reviewers in a political environment reliably validate the flaws that got the piece approved.
6. If the first review comes back "I wouldn't change a thing", you have the wrong reviewers.
7. Rehearse the **contingencies**, not just the content: an interruption, a hostile question in the middle, a technical failure, the previous speaker having made your point, the programme running thirty minutes late. Have someone deliberately disrupt a rehearsal.
8. Rehearse on the **real equipment**, in the real room, if it exists. A demonstration that has not been run in situ should be assumed to fail.
9. Manage the nerves as physiology, because that is what they are: deep breathing, movement offstage to burn the adrenaline, water shortly before, food an hour before, and the purpose of the talk written where you will see it. Acknowledged nerves make audiences sympathetic; concealed nerves read as evasion. Note that overconfidence is the more dangerous condition — healthy nervousness is what makes you prepare.

**Gates.**
- Rehearsed aloud, timed, recorded at least once.
- One correction per pass.
- At least one sceptical reviewer, ideally external.
- Contingencies rehearsed, not only listed.

**Failure signals.**
- The first full run-through is the delivery.
- A demo not tested on the actual equipment.
- Rehearsal that is re-reading the deck silently.
- A reviewer who returns it with a tick.

## DLV-05 — The pre-delivery pack

**Trigger.** The day before. Produce this as an artifact.

**Procedure.** Assemble the pack the day before, as a single artifact. It contains:

- Marked script or beat list, laid out for the lectern.
- Timing plan with the ranked cut list and both endings.
- Q&A sheet: the mundane, the technical, the hostile, and the one nobody will ask. See `REF-04`.
- One-line answer to "what do you want us to do", ready for the corridor.
- Technical checklist: files in two places, fonts embedded, video on separate media, walked through on the actual machine, adapter, clicker, batteries, backup story if it all fails.
- Room notes: where you will stand, where the friendly faces are, where the clock is, whether the lectern can be moved.
- Physical: what you are wearing, decided early and rehearsed in; no jewellery that fouls a microphone; water placed.
- The handout, as a separate artifact, to be given out at the **end**.
- Who is capturing what was committed, and how it gets to them. See `ACT-01`.

**Gates.**
- The pack exists as an artifact the day before, not as intentions.
- Room, equipment and running order confirmed rather than assumed.
- A stated fallback for the technical failure, rehearsed at least once without the technology.
- A named person responsible for recording commitments made in the room.

**Failure signals.**
- Discovering the room, the equipment or the running order on arrival.
- No plan for a technical failure.
- Handouts distributed during the talk — the room reads instead of listening.
- Nobody recording the commitments made.

## Defaults and thresholds

| Decision | Default |
|---|---|
| Content vs. slot | ~60%; never more than 90% |
| Timed aloud | ≥ 2 times |
| Recorded run-throughs | ≥ 1, watched |
| Corrections per pass | 1 |
| Opening and close | memorized verbatim |
| Half-memorized | never |
| Eye contact per person | about a second |
| Friendly faces identified | 3–4, spread |
| Assigned gestures | 1 |
| Pause before starting | long enough to feel too long |
| Endings prepared | 2 |
| Demo rehearsed on real equipment | always |
