# Narrative and Story — NAR

Story is not an ornament attached to an argument. It does two things argument cannot: it **simulates** a situation so the audience learns how to act in it, and it **inspires** — supplies the energy to act. It is also, measurably, what survives the room: an audience that recalls almost none of your individual statistics recalls the stories.

The constraint that governs the whole module: **no conflict, no story.** A narrative with no dilemma is a chronology, and a chronology persuades nobody.

---

## NAR-01 — The narrative spine

**Trigger.** Any piece whose job is to change behaviour, or whose thesis is abstract.

**Procedure.**

1. Establish the three parts, in order, and never ship without the third:
   - **Character** — someone specific enough to be imagined, with sensory detail, whose goal the audience can hold.
   - **Dilemma** — a real conflict with a cost either way. This is where the tension comes from.
   - **Resolution** — what actually happened, and the insight it yields.
   A story with tension and no resolution does not motivate; it paralyses. It leaves the audience focused on the threat rather than on the move.
2. Choose the conflict deliberately. The usual available kinds: two courses of action; two groups of people; two ideas; two objectives that cannot both be met; the old way versus the new way; a widely held belief versus what actually happens.
3. Make the audience the protagonist and the speaker the mentor. The speaker's obligations are exactly three: **guidance, confidence, and tools**. A piece that describes what the audience must do *for the speaker* has the roles inverted, and a "why this matters to you" slide at the end does not fix it.
4. Apply the **arc test**: compare the first minute to the last on `AUD-01`'s four levels — what the audience *understands*, what it *believes*, what it *feels*, and what it will *do*. Name the level that moves. If none of the four differs between minute one and the end, the piece is broken regardless of how well written it is.
5. **Show, do not tell.** Find one scene with two people in it. "Unleashing a new wave of rising social mobility" is telling; a specific person in a specific room is showing.
6. Alternate altitude between the **global** (the stakes, the pattern) and the **personal** (one person, one moment). The **institutional** level — committees, workstreams, governance bodies, programme names — is a no-fly zone. Bad drafts live there almost entirely.

**Gates.**
- Character, dilemma and resolution all present.
- The conflict is named and real.
- The audience, not the speaker, is the protagonist.
- The arc test passes.

**Failure signals.**
- A story you love that makes no point.
- Abstractions and "waves" and "journeys" with nobody in them.
- The resolution left implicit "because they'll get it".
- Everything at the institutional altitude.

## NAR-02 — Anecdote gate

**Trigger.** Before any story, case or example enters a draft.

**Procedure.** Every anecdote must pass all five:

1. **True**, or transparently presented as illustrative. A fabricated story attributed as your own is now trivially checkable and unrecoverable when caught. Admitting a story is apocryphal makes you *more* credible, not less.
2. **Revealing** about the subject, the speaker, or the occasion.
3. **Interesting** or amusing on its own terms.
4. **Simple** enough to grasp on first hearing.
5. **Tied to a point.** This is the criterion most often violated, and the reason most anecdotes should be cut.

Then:

6. Build an **on-ramp and an off-ramp**: link in, tell it, and then state the larger meaning in your own voice. Without the off-ramp the audience keeps the feeling and loses the claim.
7. Get the **level of detail** right. The odd small detail nobody else would have remembered is exactly what makes a passage feel true — keep it. Detail that is merely inventory drags the passage down; replace it with one or two broad strokes.
8. **Spot, do not invent.** Real stories are found, not constructed. You can extract a moral from a story; you cannot extract a story from a moral.
9. Match the plot to the behaviour you want:

   | Behaviour wanted | Plot |
   |---|---|
   | Persistence against odds | challenge |
   | Cooperation, reconnection, tolerance | connection |
   | New methods, invention | creativity |
   | Self-directed buy-in for a change | the single instance of the future already happening — then stop |

**Gates.**
- All five criteria pass.
- On-ramp and off-ramp both written.
- Plot matches the intended behaviour.

**Failure signals.**
- You cannot state the tie-in in one sentence.
- A story that ends and the speaker moves on without saying what it meant.
- A cooperation story used to ask for individual courage.

## NAR-03 — Simulation over instruction

**Trigger.** The audience needs judgement, not information — they must recognize a situation, not execute a known fix.

**Procedure.**

1. Narrate the situation **as it unfolded**, including the misleading cues and the dead ends, rather than publishing the extracted rule. Expertise is recognition; a bullet list does not transfer it.
2. Simulate **events, not outcomes**. Have the audience replay how the situation arose step by step. Rehearsing the process outperforms visualizing the happy ending — measurably and consistently.
3. Give them the building blocks, not your distilled recommendation. A story engages the audience's own internal voice and lets each listener customize it to their own situation. An argument invites rebuttal; a story invites extrapolation.
4. Accept the trade: you lose credit for the idea. That is the mechanism working, not a failure.
5. Where the case is intricate, use the **mystery sequence** — pose the puzzle, deepen it, raise and reject the alternative explanations, supply the clue, resolve, then state the implication. This is how you get an audience to attend to dull but decisive detail. It costs time up front and collapses if the payoff is thin.
6. Where the conclusion is counter-intuitive, let the audience reach the verdict a beat before you state it.

**Gates.**
- The narration includes the wrong turns, not only the answer.
- The audience can extrapolate to their own case.
- Any mystery structure has a payoff proportional to the setup.

**Failure signals.**
- A rule presented where a case was needed.
- A puzzle posed and never resolved.
- "Visualize success" as the intervention.

## NAR-04 — The memorable moment

**Trigger.** Any piece that must be retold, quoted, or survive the week.

**Procedure.**

1. Engineer exactly **one** moment designed to be remembered, placed at a contrast peak or at the ask. One of:
   - a **dramatization** the audience physically sees or does
   - a **repeatable line** short enough to be quoted verbatim
   - an **evocative visual**, usually a juxtaposed pair
   - an **emotive story**, often revisited at the close
   - a **startling statistic**, reframed to human scale and paired with an image
2. Verify it **magnifies** the thesis. A moment that upstages the idea has cost you the idea. If it fails the magnification test, cut it however good it is.
3. Rehearse the timing. A memorable moment that misfires is worse than none.
4. Match it to the audience's temperament. A stunt that reads as bold to one room reads as unserious to another, and the analytical room is the one that punishes it.
5. Do not flag your own quotable line. A line marked as quotable gets shared less.

**Gates.**
- Exactly one such moment.
- It magnifies rather than competes with the thesis.
- Rehearsed at least once in real conditions.

**Failure signals.**
- Two memorable moments — they cancel.
- A prop that requires equipment you have not tested.
- The moment is the only thing anyone remembers, and the thesis is not attached to it.

---

## Defaults and thresholds

| Decision | Default |
|---|---|
| Story parts | 3 — character, dilemma, resolution; never fewer |
| Memorable moments per piece | exactly 1 |
| Anecdote gate | all 5 criteria, no exceptions |
| Altitude mix | mostly global + personal; institutional is a no-fly zone |
| Story vs. statistic recall | stories survive the room; individual statistics do not |
| Personal stories to keep on hand | ~10, each mapped to an idea you argue often |

---

## Building the standing material

Stories are a stock, not a per-talk cost. Build it once:

1. Graph your own working life like a price chart and annotate the peaks and troughs.
2. Write out ten things you actually believe about how work goes.
3. Match each belief to the specific incident that produced it, and interrogate the incident until it is tellable in ninety seconds.

Personal stories are the one kind of material nobody else can copy, and disclosure is what makes identification possible. The limit: disclose only what is genuinely yours to disclose, and never manufacture the encounter.
