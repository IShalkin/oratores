# Audience and Intent — AUD

The first module, and the only one that is never optional. Nothing downstream — thesis, structure, evidence, register, length, form — is defined until the audience and the intended change are.

The recurring error this module exists to prevent: treating the *topic* as the brief. "The CEO is speaking to staff about the reorganization" is not a brief. "The CEO will use this speech to stop high performers from resigning during the reorganization" is.

---

## AUD-01 — Communication objective

**Trigger.** Any Design, Draft or Rehearse task. Run before anything else.

**Procedure.**

1. Write the objective as a single sentence naming **who** and **what change**: `<speaker> will use <this piece> to get <named audience> to <specific change>`. If you cannot complete that sentence, you do not yet have a brief, and drafting will produce a competent piece aimed at nothing.
2. Separate the four things people routinely collapse, because each takes different material:
   - what the audience must **understand** (they currently cannot follow it)
   - what they must **believe** (they follow it and do not accept it)
   - what they must **feel** (they accept it and do not care)
   - what they must **do** (they care and still will not move)
3. Name the **organizational or external outcome** that follows if the change happens, and the **success metric** — the observable thing that will be different. If no metric exists, say so explicitly rather than inventing one.
4. Match content to the type of change. This removes taste from content selection:

   | Change sought | Material that moves it |
   |---|---|
   | Thinking | facts, statistics, comparisons, third-party endorsement |
   | Feeling | stories, images, metaphor, one named person |
   | Behaviour | worked examples, role models, a scripted first step, rewards, visible peers |

5. Keep the ask **small and cumulative**. Move the audience one district, not across the country. A speech that asks for a plausible next step usually gets it; a speech that asks for conversion gets applause and nothing else.

**Gates.**
- The objective names a specific audience and a specific change, in one sentence.
- The four levels (understand / believe / feel / do) are distinguished, and you know which one is actually blocking.
- Either a success metric exists, or its absence is stated.

**Failure signals.**
- The objective is a topic ("AI adoption", "the reorganization", "Q3 results").
- The objective is about the speaker ("the CEO wants to look decisive").
- "Raise awareness" with no acceptance or action step behind it — the classic dead campaign.
- The ask is a total transformation and the audience has one hour.

## AUD-02 — Audience map and segmentation

**Trigger.** Any audience larger or more varied than one person, and any audience whose response you cannot already predict.

**Procedure.**

1. List **every** audience, primary and secondary: the people in the room, the people who will read the coverage, the people the attendees will report to, and the people who will be sent the recording. Decide explicitly whom you are willing to lose.
2. Profile the primary audience on beliefs, values, interests and incentives (what they are measured on), demographics, and situation (what happened to them this week). Values are fixed and inherited; opinions float. That distinction decides everything in AUD-03.
3. Segment by **behaviour toward your ask**, not by job title. A workable default set, when the ask is adoption of something new:

   | Segment | What they think | What convinces them | What they will actually do |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | Enthusiasts | already convinced, want more | novelty, technical access | volunteer, over-commit |
   | Early adopters | see strategic advantage | being the visible model others watch | sponsor something ambitious |
   | Pragmatic majority | "show me it works here" | in-context references from peers like them | a bounded pilot with a known owner |
   | Cautious | risk, quality, control, liability | explicit boundaries and a reversible step | agree to a limited trial with guardrails |
   | Open skeptics | "we have heard this before" | conceded limits, real failures named | stop blocking; sometimes test the claim |
   | Passive watchers | waiting to see who wins | social proof, peer visibility | follow once the norm is visible |
   | Blockers | active interest in the status quo | rarely convinced by the talk | neutralize, do not target |

   For each segment you will actually address, name: what they want, what they fear, what they do not trust, what evidence they accept, what action is realistic for them, what language to use, and what language to avoid.
4. Pick the **one segment that most influences adoption** and write primarily to them, keeping enough general content that the rest are not alienated. Targeting everyone equally persuades no one.
5. Remember the inversion: the people with the **greatest need** for a change usually adopt **last**, and the people who adopt first need it least. Plan for it rather than being surprised by it.
6. Find the **overlap** — shared experience, shared goals, and your standing with them — and speak from inside it. It cannot be manufactured from nothing, so where there is little, look in the places that reliably hold some: what this audience is measured on, what they are blamed for, a constraint you both work inside, a date or a place you both know, and the last thing that went wrong for both of you. One real item beats three plausible ones.

**Gates.**
- Secondary audiences are listed, and the ones you are willing to lose are named.
- Segments are behavioural, and each addressed segment has a realistic action attached.
- One primary segment is chosen and stated.

**Failure signals.**
- "Internal and external stakeholders" as the audience.
- Segmentation by seniority or business unit with no behavioural difference attached.
- A single message for an audience you have just described as split.
- No named fear anywhere in the map. Every real audience has one.

## AUD-03 — Resistance and anchoring

**Trigger.** The ask costs the audience something, or they are invested in the current arrangement.

**Procedure.**

1. Inventory the resistance in three kinds, because each has a different answer:
   - **Logical** — they think you are wrong. Answer with evidence and a conceded counter-case.
   - **Emotional** — the idea violates a bias, an identity, a professional self-image, or a moral code. Evidence makes this worse.
   - **Practical** — it is genuinely hard, expensive, badly timed, or someone else's decision. Acknowledge the cost and carry part of it.
2. Name the **sacrifice** explicitly: what they give up, what risk they carry, and who will be blamed if it fails. An unnamed sacrifice is the most common reason a room agrees and does nothing.
3. **Anchor, do not argue.** Values sit at the bottom of the pool and opinions float on top. Never attack a value — you will get out of your depth. Instead attach the change you want to a value they already hold, and let the juxtaposition do the work. Mere adjacency is often enough.
4. **Inoculate**: state the strongest objection yourself, before they raise it, in its strongest form. This buys credibility that no amount of assertion can. The limit: do not raise doubts the audience did not hold and would not have found.
5. Use the two levers, in this order — **raise the cost of doing nothing**, then **lower the cost of the first step**. Most drafts do only the first, which produces anxiety and paralysis rather than movement.

**Gates.**
- All three resistance kinds are considered and the dominant one is named.
- The sacrifice is stated in the piece, not only in the plan.
- At least one value the audience already holds is identified and used as the anchor.

**Failure signals.**
- Answering an emotional objection with a chart.
- A draft where nothing is conceded anywhere.
- A "why this matters" section that names benefits to the organization and none to the people in the room.
- Fear with no feasible action attached — this suppresses the message entirely rather than motivating it.

## AUD-04 — Cross-register and cross-culture fit

**Trigger.** The audience differs from the speaker in language, professional vocabulary, national culture, or seniority.

**Procedure.**

1. Audit the draft for terms that are transparent inside the speaker's world and opaque outside it. Translate rather than delete — dropping a central term leaves a hole; explaining it once buys the rest of the talk.
2. Use only the tools the audience already has. Build every new idea on something they already hold, then state the delta. An idea assembled from parts they do not possess cannot be assembled at all.
3. Check every metaphor, joke, sporting reference, historical allusion and idiom against the audience's actual world. Borrowed humour and unfamiliar references are the single most reliable way to misfire.
4. Check emotional latitude. The amount of feeling a room will accept varies enormously by culture, industry and seniority; the same close reads as moving in one room and as manipulation in another. When uncertain, under-play: an audience forgives restraint and punishes performance.
5. For interpreted delivery, prepare roughly **half** the material for consecutive interpretation, send the text well in advance, and brief the interpreter on the terms that must not be paraphrased.

**Gates.**
- No untranslated jargon carries a load-bearing claim.
- Every metaphor and reference is checked against this audience.
- The emotional register is stated in the brief, together with the register it was chosen over.

**Failure signals.**
- Acronyms outnumbering ordinary words.
- A joke that requires knowing a domestic television programme.
- "As we all know" attached to something only the speaker's team knows.

## AUD-05 — The brief, and the missing-input gate

**Trigger.** Before drafting prose or slides.

**Procedure.**

1. Fill the brief. Every field, or an explicit assumption in its place:

   ```
   speaker:                    (and their standing with this audience)
   occasion / date / venue:
   duration / slot position:
   language / register:
   primary audience:
   secondary audiences:
   what they already believe:
   what they have already heard on this:
   dominant resistance:
   understand / believe / feel / do:
   core thesis:                (MSG-01)
   evidence available:
   attributability / publicity:
   emotional latitude:
   success metric:
   ```

2. Where a field is unknown and would change the work, **ask — at most seven questions, ranked by how much the answer changes the deliverable.** Do not ask what you could infer. **Ask only where an answer can reach you:** in a forked, background or otherwise non-interactive run there is no return path, so a question ends the work instead of pausing it. There, skip to step 3 for every unknown field.
3. Where the user cannot or will not answer, choose the defensible default, write the assumption at the top of the deliverable, and produce the complete work anyway. Blocking the whole deliverable on a question is almost never correct; the exception is a factual claim you would have to invent.
4. Interview the principal when writing for someone else, and interview them **adversarially** — argue the opposite case — to surface what they actually believe and, just as important, how they actually talk. See `ETH-04`.
5. Screen the invitation itself before accepting: does this occasion advance the objective, strengthen a relationship that matters, or reach an audience worth reaching? If none, the right answer is to decline or renegotiate. See `OCC-01`.

**Gates.**
- Every brief field is either filled or carries a stated assumption.
- No more than seven questions were asked, and none was asked in a context that cannot answer.
- A complete deliverable exists even where answers were refused.

**Failure signals.**
- A question list instead of a deliverable.
- Assumptions made silently and discovered later by the speaker on stage.
- A brief with no "what they have already heard" — the field that decides whether the opening can work at all.

## Defaults and thresholds

| Decision | Default |
|---|---|
| Key audiences addressed | 2–5; more only for a genuinely complex issue |
| Segments written to | 1 primary, others not alienated |
| Questions asked before drafting | ≤ 7 |
| Falloff to plan for | ~80% reached → ~40% accepting → ~15–20% acting |
| Ask size | one step, reversible, inside their authority |
| "Eliminate" as a target | never a legitimate direction; the best case is *minimize* |
| Aim for 100% of an audience | never |
