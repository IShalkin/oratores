# Organizational Activation — ACT

Turning a piece of communication into a change that outlives the room. This module exists because the most common outcome of a good executive talk is **enthusiastic agreement followed by nothing**, and that outcome is designed in: a piece that ends in inspiration has no mechanism attached to it.

The governing distinction, and the one this module enforces everywhere:

| Not the same thing | And not the same thing |
|---|---|
| informing ≠ persuading | agreement ≠ adoption |
| persuading ≠ manipulating | a demo ≠ a proof |
| inspiring ≠ changing an organization | a pilot ≠ a capability |
| interest in a subject ≠ changed behaviour | a mandate ≠ a practice |

A speech is part of a change system or it is a media event. Decide which you are building.

---

## ACT-01 — Commitment design

**Trigger.** Boundary 2 with an organizational scope: the piece must produce action by people who will leave the room and do something.

**Procedure.**

1. End on a **commitment**, not on inspiration. For each leader in the audience, the piece must make the following answerable:

   ```
   What will I decide?
   What will I select?
   Who will I appoint?
   What will I launch?
   What will I measure?
   When will I report back?
   ```

   If a person in the audience cannot answer all six on the way out, the piece has not activated anything.
2. Make the first commitment **bounded, reversible, measurable and inside their existing authority.** A commitment that requires someone else's budget is not a commitment; it is a referral. The default shape:

   ```
   Within 30 days:
     - select one workflow or use case
     - name one accountable owner
     - define one measurable baseline
     - launch one controlled experiment
     - set the explicit risk boundary
     - report the result
   ```

3. Do not ask for a transformation. Ask for one contained, honest step, and design the mechanism that carries the second step. Scale is a consequence of a repeated first step, not a starting instruction.
4. Make the commitment **active, freely chosen, effortful, and visible.** Those four properties are what make it survive the week — and the last is the one people skip. Written by the person themselves, shown to someone else, with a small cost attached, taken under the weakest sufficient reason. Large incentives and threats produce compliance without belief, and the compliance stops when the pressure does.
5. The speaker commits first, publicly and specifically. An ask that the person asking has not already done is a request; an ask they have done is an invitation.
6. Attach the commitment to a **cue at the point of decision**, not to good intentions. A reminder placed where the choice actually happens outperforms any amount of resolve formed in a conference hall.
7. Supply the **artifact**. Nobody converts a speech into a plan on the train home. Hand over: the one-page commitment sheet, the criteria for selecting a use case, the risk boundary template, and the reporting format. See `ACT-03`.

**Gates.**
- All six commitment questions answerable by an attendee.
- The first step is inside their authority and reversible.
- The commitment is written by them, not by you.
- A physical or digital artifact exists.

**Failure signals.**
- A close that says "I encourage all of you to…".
- An ask that requires a budget decision nobody in the room owns.
- No date anywhere.
- Enthusiastic agreement in the room and no named owner.
- The ask is "start your transformation".

## ACT-02 — Visible progress and early wins

**Trigger.** Any programme lasting longer than the enthusiasm the talk generated.

**Procedure.**

1. Creating a win is **active work, not a consequence.** Put the first visible result into the plan, with a budget and an owner, before the programme starts.
2. A real win is **visible, unambiguous, and clearly attributable to the change.** A good meeting is not a win. A completed workshop is not a win. A circulated document is not a win. Only something that would still look like a result to a sceptic counts.
3. Timing: expect the first unambiguous win in roughly **six months in a small unit, eighteen in a large organization**, and plan a second before momentum decays — roughly a year after the first. A programme with nothing measurable at eighteen months has failed regardless of activity.
4. Fix the **measurement system** that would otherwise fail to record the win. This is a real and routine failure: the result happens, nothing in the reporting captures it, and the programme is judged to have produced nothing.
5. Never fake a win. A contrived result cannot carry the later stages, and it teaches the organization that the reporting is decorative.
6. Hope decays into cynicism without visible movement. The reward system detects stalling as accurately as it detects progress, which means a vision announced without a way to show movement against it is worse than no vision.
7. **Spend the credibility, do not bank it.** Immediately after a win: celebrate the specific thing briefly, then launch the next and larger step, bring in more people, and keep senior attention on it. Momentum lost before the job is done tends to reverse. Note who offers you the victory narrative — the people who want the programme to stop are the ones most eager to declare it complete.
8. The more sceptics there are, the **more** short-term wins matter — not fewer.

**Gates.**
- The first win is planned, budgeted and owned.
- It is visible, unambiguous and attributable.
- The measurement system can actually record it.
- The next step is launched from the win, not after a pause.

**Failure signals.**
- "We held the kickoff" reported as progress.
- A win announced that nobody outside the programme would recognize.
- A celebration followed by three quiet months.
- Metrics that cannot detect the thing the programme changes.

## ACT-03 — The cascade

**Trigger.** Boundary 10: the message must travel down and outward, carried by people who were in the room.

**Procedure.**

1. Design the whole chain, and name what is required at each link. The chain fails at whichever link has no owner:

   ```
   executive message
     → leadership commitment          (ACT-01)
     → business-unit interpretation
     → a named local champion
     → one selected use case
     → a bounded, measured pilot
     → a published result
     → a reusable playbook
     → replication in adjacent units
     → a routine capability
   ```

2. For each leader who must carry it onward, specify:
   - what they **receive** — the message in a form they can repeat, the selection criteria, the boundary, the reporting format
   - what they **say** to their own team, and in their own words
   - what **support** exists and who provides it
   - which **barrier** must be removed for them, and by whom
   - what gets **published**, to whom, and when
3. Make the message **repeatable.** The test is whether an attendee can state it accurately to their team the next morning. If it needs your slides, your delivery, or your vocabulary, it will not survive one retelling. Assume you are under-communicating by an order of magnitude — and buy the volume with simplicity and a metaphor rather than with more channels.
4. Deeds outrank channels. One senior person behaving inconsistently with the message outweighs the entire communication programme. Confront that before adding a newsletter.
5. Push the same message through several different forums, repeatedly, and fund it by reclaiming the time you already own — the tradition and ego content in existing management meetings — rather than by asking for new channels.
6. **Explain the apparent inconsistencies.** Every cascade generates them, and an unexplained inconsistency is read as insincerity. Name them yourself.
7. Delegate the project management and the detailed leadership downward. You cannot personally run twenty pilots, and the attempt is why most programmes run three.
8. Build **peer proof**, not just reporting. Publish the result — including the negative result — where the next unit's leaders will see it, from the mouth of someone like them. Near-peer evidence is what moves the pragmatic majority; a central dashboard is not. See `CHG-05`.

**Gates.**
- Every link in the chain has a named owner.
- The message survives one retelling without your materials.
- Each carrying leader has receive / say / support / barrier / publish specified.
- Negative results are published too.

**Failure signals.**
- A cascade deck sent to leaders who were not in the room.
- The champion is whoever volunteered.
- A published result that is only ever positive.
- The programme runs on the sponsor's personal attention.
- "We communicated it" as evidence that it was communicated.

## ACT-04 — The coalition and the barriers

**Trigger.** The change requires people the speaker does not control.

**Procedure.**

1. A guiding group needs all four of: **position power, expertise, credibility, and leadership.** A group made entirely of competent managers will fail at this — not because they are weak, but because managing and leading are different functions, and a change is mostly the second. Score the roster on all four and fix the gap explicitly.
2. Keep out the two kinds of member who cost more than they contribute: the person who leaves no room for anyone else, and the person who destroys trust. If exclusion is politically impossible, manage them closely and say so.
3. Build the group on trust, deliberately: shared work on real external data, plus enough time together to matter, plus the dozens of small actions between — confronting a rumour immediately, pairing the people who know each other least.
4. When believers deliver nothing, it is an **arrangement problem, not a motivation problem.** Audit four barriers:
   - **Structure** — does the organization fragment the resources the change needs?
   - **Skills** — is the gap technical, or is it attitude and confidence?
   - **Systems** — do information, appraisal, promotion and pay reward the old behaviour?
   - **Supervisors** — is there a manager who will not let go?
   Nothing disempowers people the way a bad immediate boss does, and no communication programme outruns one.
5. Handle a blocking manager with **honest dialogue**, not manoeuvre: state the situation, the objective, the specific help needed, and the timeframe — then ask what would make it possible for them to help. Defensible in daylight, fast-clarifying, and if they are willing but unable, the explicit expectations make the eventual change less contentious.
6. Prune the interdependencies that make the change expensive. Who actually needs this report, at this frequency, at this approval threshold, and why? Most of the answer is historical.

**Gates.**
- The coalition scores on all four requirements.
- The four barriers audited, not assumed away.
- Blocking managers addressed directly and on the record.

**Failure signals.**
- "Who is pushing for this?" answered with "mostly me" — you are at the beginning, not the middle.
- A steering group of managers with no proven change leaders.
- "You should be better team players" offered in response to a structural complaint.
- Training aimed only at managers.

## ACT-05 — Making it routine

**Trigger.** The pilot worked and you want the practice to persist.

**Procedure.**

1. Nothing is adopted until it is **routine** — embedded in the process, the tooling, the appraisal, the onboarding, and the way work is described. A practice that depends on someone remembering it is not adopted; it is being performed.
2. The sequence is fixed and cannot be run in reverse:
   1. let the new practice produce a clear result
   2. talk explicitly and repeatedly about the causal link between the new behaviour and the result — and about why the old way made sense when it started, and no longer does
   3. check that hiring is not screening against the new norm
   4. stop promoting people who do not hold the new practice
   5. manage succession deliberately, including whoever oversees the programme
   6. accept that some turnover may be required
3. **Culture goes last, not first.** It changes after behaviour changes, after the behaviour produces a benefit, and after people see the connection. Any programme whose step one is "change the culture" should be treated as suspect.
4. Where the practice reverses, classify it: **replacement** by something better is fine and is not a failure; **abandonment** out of disenchantment means the thing or its support was wrong, and that is yours to fix.
5. Reinforce approximations continuously — with **recognition**, not with reward. Notice the smallest recognizable piece of the target behaviour every time it appears, rather than waiting for the finished version. `ACT-01`'s weakest-sufficient-reason rule stays in force while you do it: a material reward attached to a behaviour after adoption is exactly what removes the self-attributed reason that makes it stick, which is why the early-adoption incentive in `CHG-05` is withdrawn rather than extended. Most people are stingy reinforcers by default and generous complainers.
6. Then let it compound. Familiarity and self-justification both accrue with **time** rather than with success, so inertia eventually switches from opponent to ally — but only after the early reinforcement has been paid for.

**Gates.**
- The practice is embedded in at least one system, not only in behaviour.
- The causal link is stated aloud, repeatedly.
- Culture work follows results rather than preceding them.
- Reversals are classified rather than lamented.

**Failure signals.**
- The practice decays when the sponsor moves on.
- A culture programme running ahead of any result.
- The result achieved and the promotion criteria unchanged.
- "I've done my share, now it's someone else's turn."

## Defaults and thresholds

| Decision | Default |
|---|---|
| Commitment horizon | 30 days for the first step |
| Commitment scope | one workflow, one owner, one baseline, one boundary |
| First unambiguous win | ~6 months small unit / ~18 months large organization |
| Second win | within roughly a year of the first |
| Under-communication assumption | you are off by an order of magnitude |
| Message repeatability test | an attendee retells it accurately, unaided, next morning |
| Coalition requirements | all four: position, expertise, credibility, leadership |
| Culture work | last |
| Pilots run on the sponsor's attention | as few as possible; delegate |
| Negative results published | always |
