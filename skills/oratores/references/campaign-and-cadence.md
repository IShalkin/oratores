# Campaign and Cadence — CMP

When one piece is not the unit of work. A single talk almost never changes anything on its own; what changes things is a message repeated with variation, across channels, over a period, by more than one person. This module handles that — and the fact that most professional communication fails not because a piece was bad but because there was only one of it.

---

## CMP-01 — The series, and one core message

**Trigger.** Boundary 10: the message will be repeated, cascaded, or delivered by more than one person.

**Procedure.**

1. Keep **one core message** across the whole series and vary everything else — the text, the argument, the examples, the emphasis, the delivery. Manage the momentum of the series, not the quality of the next piece.
2. **Recycle deliberately.** Reuse of proven material is correct practice, not laziness: repetition is how a speaker becomes fluent and how an audience becomes familiar. Staff boredom is not audience overexposure, and the people closest to a message are always the first to be sick of it — including about the lines that turn out to be the ones that land.
3. Build **reusable modules**: a stock of stories, one per idea you argue often; the standing objection answers; the two or three numbers you can always defend; the one memorable line. Then assemble rather than write from scratch.
4. Develop one **signature piece** at the overlap of what you know and what your audiences want, and deliver it repeatedly until it runs without effort — about five deliveries. Every historically effective speech was pre-tested many times before the version anyone remembers.
5. Assume you are **under-communicating by an order of magnitude.** Buy the volume with simplicity and one metaphor rather than with more channels, and fund it by reclaiming time you already own rather than by requesting new airtime.
6. Push the same message through several different forums, small and large, formal and informal. Repetition across forms is what produces belief that repetition within one form does not.
7. Explain the apparent inconsistencies as you go. A series generates them, and an unexplained one is read as insincerity.
8. Vary the frequency deliberately — continuous, in bursts, in pulses, or massed at one moment. Pick a pattern rather than defaulting to whenever someone asks.

**Gates.**
- One core message, stated identically enough to be recognized.
- Reusable modules exist, so pieces are assembled rather than written.
- The volume assumption is an order of magnitude, not a small multiple.
- Inconsistencies named rather than hoped over.

**Failure signals.**
- A new theme every quarter.
- Six pieces a week and no strategy — a production line rather than a campaign.
- "We already said that" as the reason not to say it again.
- New channels requested before existing ones are used properly.

## CMP-02 — Planning the cadence

**Trigger.** Designing a programme of communication rather than a piece.

**Procedure.**

1. Work the order, and do not reverse it. Each step is only defensible if the previous one was done:

   ```
   situation  →  audiences  →  objectives  →  message strategy
              →  channels and tactics  →  schedule and budget  →  evaluation
   ```

   Audiences before objectives. Objectives before strategy. Strategy before tactics. Tactics before budget. Evaluation designed **before** implementation.
2. State the situation as a **noun phrase with no verb and no judgement**, and classify it as an opportunity or an obstacle. Verbs belong to the objectives. This one discipline stops a preferred solution from being smuggled into the problem statement.
3. **Fix performance before message.** Communication cannot promote something that does not work, and reputation follows performance rather than words. Where the audience's negative impression is *accurate*, the work is to change the thing, not the description. This is the most defensible move available in this whole module and the one most often skipped.
4. Choose two to five key audiences. More only for a genuinely complex issue. For each, place them on the path from unaware → aware → accepting → acting, and write at least one objective at each level they need to cross.
5. **Never skip acceptance.** Jumping from awareness to action is the classic failure: a programme with an awareness objective and an action objective and nothing in between will report reach and produce nothing.
6. Write each objective with the audience, the level, the direction, the specific effect, the metric, and the time period. Then back-solve the numbers from real capacity rather than from ambition: start from how many the organization could actually absorb, and work upward. The resulting percentages will look small and will be the correct ones.
7. Choose channels by what each actually supplies:

   | You need | Channel type | Note |
   |---|---|---|
   | Control of content and timing | your own | no third-party credibility |
   | Third-party credibility | earned | requires something genuinely newsworthy |
   | Peer credibility | shared | go only where these people actually talk; staff it before launching it |
   | Reach without credibility | paid | most expensive, and does not persuade |
   | **Acceptance** | interpersonal | least reach, most effect — and the best route to acceptance |

   Work from your own channels outward and buy last.
8. Reach and effect trade off inversely. A programme whose whole plan is high-reach channels has no acceptance mechanism, which is the same failure as (5) arriving by a different route.
9. Annotate every candidate activity with the audience it serves, its likely effect, and its cost. That annotation is the budget.

**Gates.**
- The order is unbroken.
- Situation stated as a noun.
- Performance problems separated from message problems.
- An acceptance objective exists for every audience that needs one.
- Evaluation designed before implementation.

**Failure signals.**
- Tactics chosen first and objectives written backwards from them.
- An objective that names an activity ("produce a video").
- "Educate", "inform", "promote", "encourage" as the objective.
- Awareness plus action with no acceptance.
- A target of 100% of anything.

## CMP-03 — Measurement and honesty about effect

**Trigger.** Anyone is about to claim the programme worked.

**Procedure.**

1. Design the evaluation before you implement. Retrofitted measurement measures whatever is available, which is never the thing that mattered.
2. Measure at all the levels, and keep them distinct:
   - **outputs** — what you produced and distributed
   - **outtakes** — what the audience noticed and took away
   - **awareness** — what they now know
   - **acceptance** — what they now think or feel
   - **action** — what they now do
   Only the last is the point. Distinguish it clearly from the others, because outputs are the easiest to report and the least informative.
3. **Behaviour is the ultimate metric.** A programme can achieve very high awareness while the behaviour it exists to change moves in the wrong direction, and that is a failed programme regardless of the awareness figure.
4. Do not report a **count as coverage**, or **reach as effect**. An exposure number tells you how many people could have noticed, and nothing about whether anything changed. A green report tells you the things already being tracked are still fine; it says nothing about the class of failure nobody has thought of.
5. Discount figures supplied by anyone with an interest in the result, including your own. Do not read press comment or a published poll as the *target audience's* reaction.
6. Never convert editorial coverage into an equivalent advertising value, and never apply a multiplier to it. Editorial matter is not free advertising, and cost is not value.
7. Ask the question that most evaluations avoid: **would this outcome have happened anyway?** A campaign that rode an existing trend looks powerful while doing almost no work; a campaign that pushed against one fails regardless of budget. Establish which you were doing before claiming the result.
8. Measure the persistence, not just the peak. Whether behaviour survives after the programme stops is the only distinction that matters between an effect and an event.
9. Report the negative result. A programme that only ever publishes successes teaches the organization that its own reporting is decorative, which costs more than the failure did.

**Gates.**
- Evaluation designed before implementation.
- All five levels measured and kept distinct.
- No count reported as coverage, no reach reported as effect.
- The counterfactual question asked explicitly.
- Negative results published.

**Failure signals.**
- "We posted a lot" as the achievement.
- Rising engagement with falling trust reported as success.
- An advertising-equivalent figure anywhere.
- The measurement instrument invented after the programme.
- No answer to "would this have happened anyway?"

## Defaults and thresholds

| Decision | Default |
|---|---|
| Core messages per campaign | 1 |
| Key audiences | 2–5 |
| Objectives per audience | at least one at each level it must cross |
| Acceptance objective | mandatory |
| Falloff to plan for | ~80% aware → ~40% accepting → ~15–20% acting |
| Under-communication assumption | an order of magnitude |
| Signature piece repetitions to fluency | ~5 |
| Channel order | own → earned → shared → paid |
| Evaluation design | before implementation |
| Advertising value equivalence | never |
| 100% of an audience | never a target |
