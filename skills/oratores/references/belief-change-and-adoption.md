# Belief Change and Adoption — CHG

What to do when agreement is not the objective. This module is for the gap between a room that nods and an organization that behaves differently — and for the fact that the gap is not a communication failure. It is usually a clarity failure, a situation failure, or a capacity failure wearing the costume of resistance.

The three diagnoses to run **before** blaming anyone:

1. What looks like a **people problem** is usually a **situation problem**.
2. What looks like **laziness** is usually **exhaustion**. Self-control is an exhaustible resource, and a large change taxes exactly the faculties needed to make it.
3. What looks like **resistance** is usually a **lack of clarity**. Not "be more data-driven" — "next time you write a client proposal, put the baseline number in the second paragraph."

---

## CHG-01 — Diagnose the blockage

**Trigger.** Boundary 2: the ask is a behaviour change, and either it is not happening or you are about to ask for it.

**Procedure.** Three things must be addressed, and addressing only one paralyses.

| Element | What it needs | Its failure mode |
|---|---|---|
| **Direction** — the planning, rational part | crystal-clear, specific, observable next behaviour | spinning: endless analysis, options proliferating, "we agree but nothing happens" |
| **Motivation** — the feeling part | a felt reason and the confidence to start | short-termism; easily spooked; "they know and still don't act" |
| **Situation** — the environment, process and norms | the right behaviour made easy, the wrong one made hard | "it worked and then decayed"; "nobody else is doing it" |

Diagnose which one is blocking, using the test that actually discriminates: **would they pass a pop quiz on what to do?** If no, it is direction. If yes, and still nothing happens, it is motivation or situation.

Symptom table:

| What you observe | Element | First move |
|---|---|---|
| "I don't know where to start"; options multiplying | direction | find what is already working; script the specific move |
| "We agree, but nothing happens" | direction, not motivation | reduce the goal to one observable behaviour with a time and place |
| "They know and still don't act" | motivation | make them see something, not read something |
| Dread; avoidance; "it's too much" | motivation (confidence) | shrink the change until it can be stepped over |
| Quitting at the first failure | motivation (mindset) | create the expectation of failure en route; frame it as learning |
| Rationalizing partial compliance | direction | a goal with no room to argue around it |
| The right behaviour is inconvenient | situation | go watch where the friction is, and remove it |
| It worked, then decayed | situation | build a habit; attach an explicit trigger |
| "Nobody else is doing it" | situation (norms) | make the favourable norm visible; never publicize an unfavourable one |
| Entrenched culture resists a mandate | situation (culture) | give the reformers a private, recurring space and their own vocabulary |

**Gates.**
- The three pre-diagnoses run before any attribution of resistance.
- The blocking element named.
- The intervention names the element it targets, and the elements ruled out.

**Failure signals.**
- Building a deck for a motivation problem.
- Reaching for incentives — usually a sign of running out of imagination rather than of options.
- Labelling people (resistant, laggards, blockers, dinosaurs) — that is a situation you have not looked at.
- A goal containing an adjective rather than a verb: "be more innovative", "have a growth mindset", "be data-led". Not yet a behaviour.

## CHG-02 — Direction: clarity, bright spots, destination

**Trigger.** The blockage is direction.

**Procedure.**

1. **Find what already works.** Look for the positive outliers — the team, office, or individual already doing the thing against the odds — and clone their *behaviour*, not their explanation. Ask "what is working, and how do we do more of it?" rather than "what is broken?". Spend most of the effort scaling success rather than diagnosing failure.
   Watch for the reflex: the first reaction to an outlier's success is usually suspicion. Suppress it.
   Watch also for analysis that is **true but useless** — correct, complete, and impossible to act on.
2. **Script the critical moves.** Ladder down from the idea to observable behaviours; choose the few with the most leverage; state each with a time and a place; and publicly ignore everything else. Over-scripting recreates the problem — script the *critical* moves only.
3. Set **behavioural** goals, not only outcome goals. Change efforts that specify behaviours succeed at a far higher rate than those that specify outcomes.
4. **Point to the destination**: a vivid near-term picture in the audience's own language, months rather than years out. A financial or SMART target presumes the motivation; it does not generate it. Get the beginning and the ending right and do not try to plot the middle.
5. Where the real risk is rationalization or quiet non-compliance, use a goal with **no room to argue** — a bright line rather than a target. Restrictive and uninspiring, and correct when someone will otherwise negotiate with the definition.
6. Reduce options. More options and more ambiguity both produce paralysis, and the default path is always the status quo.

**Gates.**
- At least one bright spot identified, or its genuine absence established.
- The ask is one observable behaviour with a time and place.
- A destination picture exists in the audience's own words.

**Failure signals.**
- A framework where a first step was needed.
- "Everyone agrees" for the third quarter running.
- A vision statement with no behaviour attached.
- Twelve recommended practices.

## CHG-03 — Motivation: see, feel, shrink, grow

**Trigger.** The blockage is motivation.

**Procedure.**

1. The sequence is **see → feel → change**, not analyse → think → change. Decide what you want people to feel, then design what they will **see**: an artifact on the table, a live demonstration, a visit to the place where the problem is, one affected person in the room, a side-by-side of the two states.
2. Choose the emotion by what the change requires — see `PTH-01`. Ambiguous problems needing invention require hope, pride and interest. A burning platform buys compliance and burns the credibility you need for the next step.
3. **Shrink the change** until it can be stepped over. Two ways, and either works:
   - limit the investment — five minutes, one workflow, one week, the smallest item first
   - show how far they have already come, so the remaining distance looks small
   Choose the small win that is meaningful *and* within immediate reach; when you must choose, take reachable. Early success is engineered hope, and hope is the fuel.
4. Build a **yardstick** — a simple 0-to-10 scale — and celebrate every single increment, including the ones that look trivial. Then ask "what would get us to the next point?"
5. **Sell an identity, not an incentive.** People decide by asking: who am I, what kind of situation is this, and what would someone like me do here? Any change that violates a professional identity will fail regardless of incentive, which is why reaching for a bonus is usually a sign that the identity work has not been done. Identities can be adopted and even invented — never by deception. The test: could you say your strategy out loud in the room without embarrassment?
6. Install the expectation of failure en route. Everything looks like a failure in the middle, and a team that has not been told this reads the middle as proof it was a bad idea. Praise effort and ingenuity; frame the work as learning rather than as performing.
7. Where self-interest is weak, absent, or insulting, switch to identity. Where identity is intact and the task feels too big, shrink. Preferably both.

**Gates.**
- Something the audience *sees*, not only reads.
- The first step is small enough that refusing it would look unreasonable.
- The identity implied by the ask is named and is one the audience would claim.
- Failure en route is explicitly predicted.

**Failure signals.**
- An analytical appeal to a motivation problem.
- Fear with an ambiguous required response.
- A staged encounter whose felt truth is not the actual truth. That is manipulation, and it is detected. See `MEC-01`.
- A milestone nobody celebrates.

## CHG-04 — Situation: friction, habits, norms

**Trigger.** The blockage is the situation. This is the most under-used family of interventions and the cheapest.

**Procedure.**

1. Go and **watch** someone attempt the behaviour. Find the friction. Then remove it from the right path and add it to the wrong one. Good situational fixes are frequently disliked at first and judged by results afterwards.
2. Where the stakes are high, make the wrong behaviour **impossible** rather than discouraged. This removes flexibility and judgement, which is the cost, and is correct when you cannot rely on attention.
3. **Build habits.** A habit is progress for free — it does not draw on anyone's attention. Habits form whether or not you intend them, so the only real question is which ones. A candidate qualifies only if it (a) advances the objective and (b) is genuinely easy. A hard "habit" is just the original problem renamed.
4. Attach an **explicit trigger**: pre-decide the exact when and where, and make the cue vivid enough to interrupt an ordinary day. Pre-deciding the moment sharply raises follow-through on hard goals.
5. Use a **checklist** for the steps that must happen every time. Keep it short and evidence-based; a bloated checklist gets ignored and discredits the idea.
6. **Make the favourable norm visible.** Behaviour is contagious, and in an unfamiliar situation people look to others for the cue. Publicize a majority norm; **never publicize a minority one** — telling people that few are complying tells them what normal is. Where no norm exists, seed one, connect the believers to each other, or create the visible in-group.
7. For culture change against entrenched practice, give the reformers a private, well-attended, recurring space, rehearsed responses, and their own vocabulary — and tolerate a temporary in-group / out-group split. Think of it as time-boxed, not as a faction.

**Gates.**
- Someone has actually watched the behaviour being attempted.
- Each habit is genuinely easy and advances the objective.
- Every asked-for behaviour has a trigger.
- No unfavourable norm is being publicized.

**Failure signals.**
- Exhortation where friction was the cause.
- A mandate issued into a process that makes compliance expensive.
- "Only 12% have started" published to the other 88%.
- A checklist nobody uses.

## CHG-05 — Diffusion: why it spreads or does not

**Trigger.** The change must spread beyond the people in the room.

**Procedure.**

1. Score the change **as the intended adopter perceives it** — not as its designer sees it. Five attributes, and together they explain most of the variance in how fast anything spreads:

   | Attribute | Question | If weak |
   |---|---|---|
   | **Relative advantage** | is it visibly better than what it replaces? | re-position, quantify, or bundle it with something already adopted. Strongest single predictor |
   | **Compatibility** | does it fit existing values, practice and felt needs? | change the form or the meaning, not just the message; find the specific incompatible value |
   | **Complexity** | how hard does it look? | simplify, chunk, and supply *how-to* knowledge — the most under-supplied kind |
   | **Trialability** | can it be tried on a limited basis? | engineer a partial trial, a sandbox, or a vicarious trial by demonstration |
   | **Observability** | are the results visible to others? | make both the adoption and the result visible; this is what most internal programmes miss |

   Attack the weakest first. If all five look fine and it still stalls, the constraint is the network, the sponsor, or the norms — not the thing.
2. Match the channel to the **stage**. A broadcast creates awareness and is nearly useless for persuasion. Persuasion and decision happen through **near-peers** — someone recognizably like the adopter who has already done it. Implementation needs how-to support. Confirmation needs reinforcement, or you get quiet abandonment.
3. Diagnose the **gap between attitude and practice**: favourable knowledge, favourable attitude, no behaviour. Do not answer it with more information. Supply a cue to act, and near-peer proof. This is the standard failure mode of anything whose benefit is delayed or probabilistic.
4. Recruit the people others watch — but only if the norms are at least moderately favourable to change. Where the prevailing norm is traditional, the respected people are **not** the innovative ones, and recruiting them stalls you.
5. Treat **local modification as a fit signal, not as deviance.** People change what they adopt as they adopt it, and that adaptation is what makes it stick. Loosen the bundle deliberately where local variation is legitimate, and be explicit about the parts that are not negotiable.
6. Where value rises with the number of other adopters, you are below critical mass until roughly a tenth to a fifth have adopted. Until then: target the respected few first, introduce it to **intact groups** rather than to individuals, make the trajectory visible, and incentivize early adoption only — then withdraw the incentive.
7. Expect the **inversion**: those with the greatest need adopt last, and the first adopters need it least. Do not read early enthusiasm as evidence about the majority.
8. Note the chasm between the people who want a change agent and the people who want a productivity improvement. Enthusiasts and early adopters buy the discontinuity; the pragmatic majority buys evolution, wants references from inside their own context, and will not debug it for you. Which means: **early adopters are structurally poor references for the majority.** You need one contained, complete, referenceable win inside a segment that references itself.
9. Classify consequences before pushing adoption: desirable/undesirable, direct/indirect, anticipated/unanticipated — and expect the undesirable, indirect and unanticipated to arrive together. Watch form, function and **meaning**; sponsors reliably see the first two and miss the third, which is where the damage is.

**Gates.**
- All five attributes scored from the adopter's perspective.
- Near-peer proof available for the persuasion stage.
- One contained, referenceable win identified.
- Consequences classified.

**Failure signals.**
- "It's obviously better, so the resistance is irrational" — look for the adopter's rational reason.
- "These people need educating."
- Adoption counted by asking the sponsor — that measures announcement, not practice.
- A programme that collapses when its champion leaves: it never became routine.
- Everyone adopted, including those who should not have.

## Defaults and thresholds

| Decision | Default |
|---|---|
| Effort split | ~80% on scaling what works, ~20% on diagnosing failure |
| Goal type | behavioural, with a time and place |
| First step size | small enough that refusal looks unreasonable |
| Yardstick | 0–10; celebrate every increment |
| Norm publication | only when the majority already complies |
| Critical mass band | ~10–20% adoption |
| Referenceable win needed before scaling | 1, complete, inside one self-referencing segment |
| Options offered | few; more options reduce action |
| Habit test | advances the objective **and** genuinely easy |
