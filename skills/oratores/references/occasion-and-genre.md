# Occasion and Genre — OCC

What the occasion permits, and what kind of piece it actually is. The classical insight this module rests on is still the most useful one available: **the genre is determined by what the hearer is doing, not by the topic.**

| What is the audience doing? | Genre | Time | The end you must argue to | Lead proof |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Deciding what to **do** | deliberative | future | what is expedient or harmful | **example and precedent** |
| Judging what was **done** | forensic | past | what is just, whose fault, what the record shows | **inference from evidence** |
| **Watching** and appraising | epideictic | present | what is admirable or shameful | **amplification** |

Two consequences that decide real drafts:

1. Arguing justice to an audience that is deciding what to do is a category error, unless you convert it into utility. Arguing utility to an audience that is judging a record is the same error in reverse.
2. Praise and counsel are convertible. When you want to praise, think what you would advise; when you want to advise, think what you would praise.

You cannot argue the future from facts about the future — you have none. You argue it from precedent. You argue the past from inference. Misdiagnosing which one you are in wastes your strongest material.

## OCC-01 — Accept, decline, or renegotiate

**Trigger.** An invitation, a slot, or an assignment arrives.

**Procedure.**

1. Screen it against the objective. Does this occasion advance the actual goal, strengthen a relationship that matters, or reach an audience worth reaching? If none, decline. Saying yes to everything produces a schedule that manufactures pieces rather than persuasion.
2. Check the structural red flags:

   | Situation | Decision |
   |---|---|
   | One of several speakers | a qualified maybe; upgrade only if your material is genuinely distinct |
   | Slot one or two | safe |
   | Slot three or later | a real cost; the room is spent |
   | Last slot | decline |
   | Topic needs thirty minutes, you have fifteen | renegotiate or decline |
   | Given forty-five minutes for thirty minutes of material | renegotiate; padding shows |
   | You would have to make a claim you cannot support | decline |
   | Any genuine reservation about the audience, the topic, or the position | decline |

   Do not make going first a condition of acceptance; it reads badly and it is rarely available.
3. Where the occasion is fixed and unsuitable, change the **form** rather than forcing the content. A twenty-minute slot for material that needs an hour is not a shorter talk; it is a different artifact plus a document. See the form ladder in `SKILL.md`.
4. Where you are one of several speakers, find out what the others are covering. Two speakers making the same point is a worse outcome than either of them not speaking.

**Gates.**
- The invitation is screened against the objective, not against availability.
- Slot position is known and its cost accepted.
- A mismatch between material and time is resolved before drafting, not during.

**Failure signals.**
- Accepting because it was asked.
- Discovering the running order on the day.
- The same content delivered at a length it was not designed for.

## OCC-02 — Fit the piece to the occasion

**Trigger.** After acceptance, before drafting.

**Procedure.**

1. Establish the register the occasion permits. Written style is the most finished and is meant to be read. Combative style is built for delivery. A deliberative address is drawing in light and shade — designed to produce its effect at a distance, and it will not bear close reading. A ceremonial piece is meant to be re-read, and should survive it.
2. Do not import the register of one occasion into another. A conference keynote delivered at a governance meeting reads as grandstanding; a governance briefing delivered at a keynote reads as a wasted slot.
3. Match the arrangement to the occasion. The default minimum is **statement plus proof**; everything else has to earn its place:

   | Part | Include when | Skip when |
   |---|---|---|
   | Opening | you need attention, goodwill, or must handle a prejudice | the subject is already known and the stakes obvious |
   | Narrative of the facts | the facts are contested, or the account carries the character | the matter is agreed, notorious, or concerns the future |
   | Proof | always | — |
   | Refutation | the audience will hear the other side, or is undecided | a friendly room hearing nothing else |
   | Close | you have something to do with the last thirty seconds | almost never |

4. Where the facts damage you, extenuate rather than dwell. Dwelling amplifies for the other side.
5. Where the narrative is obscure, fix it before anything else. Obscurity in the account of what happened spreads darkness over everything after it, and it gets only one chance.
6. Handle the standing genres with their own shapes:
   - **Introduction of a speaker**: why they are here, what qualifies them, and one story that reveals character. Two or three minutes, organized around the story rather than the biography. Never "needs no introduction"; never a read biography. Supply your own when you are being introduced — offered as a suggestion, it is usually used verbatim.
   - **Ceremonial** (retirement, award, dedication, eulogy): one revealing memory → what it shows → what the rest of us should do now. Brief, and not read.
   - **Report at a meeting**: state the general position → break it down → restate the take-away. Not a list of items. Where you have no assigned slot, prepare the problem in a sentence and two or three recommendations, then speak when the discussion has exhausted itself rather than first.
   - **Apology**: accept responsibility, act fast, be plain, and pair it with the corrective action and restitution. Regret is not apology, and non-apologetic regret reliably fails and invites escalation.
   - **Crisis**: the first message goes out fast, one trained spokesperson, and silence is almost never the right answer. "No comment" reads as guilt plus incompetence.
   - **Bad news that is coming anyway**: tell it first, with the context, if you hold the evidence. The first telling becomes the normative one. The cost is that you enlarge the audience.
7. Choose the **historical span** deliberately. "Historically" means whatever period supports the argument, and a longer span makes a trend look inevitable while a shorter one makes it look like a choice. Either can be honest; pick one and be able to defend the choice.
8. Read the moment. Where an event overtakes the occasion, strip the partisan material and speak to what the room is actually experiencing. A prepared text delivered into a changed room is the most avoidable failure in this discipline.

**Gates.**
- Register matched to the occasion.
- Each arrangement part present carries a written reason; each part omitted is named as omitted.
- Refutation present wherever the other side will be heard.
- The historical span is a defensible choice.

**Failure signals.**
- A keynote structure in a decision meeting.
- An opening on an occasion where the subject was already known and the stakes obvious.
- An account of what happened that the audience cannot follow.
- The prepared text delivered unchanged into a room that has just heard something else.

## OCC-03 — Arena and control

**Trigger.** The piece is not a set-piece you control.

**Procedure.**

1. Place the arena on the control gradient, and prepare in inverse proportion to your control:

   | Arena | Who controls the agenda | Prepare |
   |---|---|---|
   | Set-piece speech | you | the text and the delivery; rehearse the intonation |
   | Panel | shared, partly anticipable | three positions, one line each, and a plan for being agreed with |
   | Internal meeting or debate | shared | the answers, not the statement; role-play the opposition |
   | Interview or press conference | the interviewer | the hostile question, and one message you will reach regardless |
   | Q&A after your own talk | shared, but you close it | written answers across the range; see `REF-04` |

   The lowest-risk arena still gets the most drafting; the highest-risk arena gets the most rehearsal of **replies**.
2. In a low-control arena, decide the **grade of reply** in advance for each difficult question: full answer, partial answer, deferred answer, referred elsewhere, or declined. Choosing deliberately is the difference between judgement and evasion — and note that waffling costs more than a plain answer whenever anyone consequential is watching closely.
3. **Where the other side speaks in the same room, their turn is your working time, not your waiting time.** Take down their **exact wording**, not the gist. Their own words turned back on them are the one route of proof you cannot prepare in advance, and a paraphrase destroys it — `LOG-01`, `LNG-05`. Watch them as well as hear them: the movements of a face generally show the workings of the mind behind it. Do this without being seen doing it, because an opponent who notices he is being read gains the advantage you were taking.
4. Ration exposure in adversarial arenas and over-prepare the ones you cannot avoid. Scarcity will be read as evasion; that is a cost, not a reason to appear everywhere.
5. Speak past the interviewer to the audience. The interviewer's assent is not the win condition.
6. Never differ publicly from a colleague, especially one standing beside you. Align the people before you align the content.
7. Where you are cornered on damage: state the concern and the responsibility separately, and never claim a power you have declined to use.
8. Where you genuinely do not know: say so once and move on. Where you made a factual error: correct it immediately and once.
9. Debrief afterwards. The arena you handle badly twice is one you did not review the first time.

**Gates.**
- Arena identified and preparation matched to control.
- Reply grades decided in advance for the hard questions.
- In any arena where the other side speaks, their exact wording is written down rather than paraphrased.
- Colleagues aligned before the event.
- A debrief planned.

**Failure signals.**
- The same preparation for a keynote and an interview.
- An improvised answer on the one subject you knew would come up.
- Notes of the other side's case in your own words — the sentence you needed to quote back is gone.
- An opponent who can tell he is being studied.
- Two people from the same organization giving different answers on a platform.
- A holding formula used twice.

## Defaults and thresholds

| Decision | Default |
|---|---|
| Genre | decided by what the audience is doing, not by the topic |
| Minimum arrangement | statement + proof |
| Introduction of a speaker | 2–3 minutes, built around one story |
| Ceremonial talk | brief, unread, three parts |
| Crisis first message | fast; silence is almost never right |
| "No comment" | never |
| Slot position | 1 or 2; decline last |
| Preparation | inversely proportional to your control of the agenda |
| Public disagreement with a colleague | never |
| Correction of a factual error | immediately, once |
